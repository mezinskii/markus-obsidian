## The Translation

The whole text of the *Meditations* is given in this book in the translation of George Long (1800–1879), first published in 1862 as *The Thoughts of the Emperor M. Aurelius Antoninus*. The choice is a simple one. Long's version is in the public domain, it is among the most often reprinted English translations, and it is the one most readers are likely to meet. It is also a faithful translation: Long was a classical scholar, and he follows the Greek closely, often more closely than smoother modern versions.

His English, however, is the English of the 1860s. Marcus addresses himself as "thou," and Long keeps the archaic forms ("dost," "hast," "wouldst") throughout. They are left unchanged here. The reader soon grows used to them, and they have one advantage: they mark clearly where Marcus is speaking to himself.

Like every translator, Long sometimes departs from the Greek, and several such places are discussed in the commentaries. In [[03-14|3.14]] he renders τὰ ὑπομνημάτιά σου, "your little notes," as "thy own memoirs"; the introduction explains why this matters for the genre of the whole book. In [[12-03|12.3]] and [[12-36|12.36]] the same word, ἵλεως, "gracious," stands at the end of each passage, and Long renders it once as "obedient" and once as "satisfied," so that the echo on which the *Meditations* close is lost. In [[06-13|6.13]], in the manner of his age, he leaves out the sentence on sexual intercourse, which belongs to the exercise the passage describes. Where Long is more exact than one might expect, the commentaries say so too.

## The Greek Text

The Greek text has been checked against the edition of J. H. Leopold (Oxford, 1908). The *Meditations* survive in very few late manuscripts, and in a number of places editors read the text differently; such variants are called readings. Long translated from the editions available to him, and in some places he follows a reading other than Leopold's. In [[10-13|10.13]] his "if another does what is just and right" reflects a different word from Leopold's "if what is just and right is blamed by another." In [[04-33|4.33]] his "Leonnatus" is the reading of the manuscripts, which modern editors correct to Dentatus. In [[09-29|9.29]] Long, like several editors, follows the reading "no small matter," where Leopold prints "a small matter." Where such a difference affects the sense, it is noted in the commentary or in a note.

## Numbering

The passages are numbered by the standard system: the number of the book and the number of the chapter within it, so that 4.3 means Book IV, chapter 3. The division of the text into passages follows this numbering, not Long's paragraphs, so a passage sometimes begins or ends where Long has no break. In Leopold, for example, [[12-17|12.17]] and [[12-18|12.18]] are not separated; here they are divided at the boundary of the chapters, and the first passage breaks off in mid-sentence. Long's words have not been changed.

## Corrections

Long's text has not been rewritten. Where his translation departs from the sense of the Greek, this is discussed in a commentary or a note, not silently corrected: the reader sees the translator's words and knows where they cannot be trusted.

Only errors introduced when the old text was transcribed have been corrected, and only where the Project Gutenberg edition of Long confirms the correction: in [[05-09|5.9]], for example, "bast" has been restored to "hast," and in [[05-08|5.8]] "precribed" to "prescribed." Long's dashes, which older transcriptions render in different ways, are given throughout as dashes. Long's square brackets, his own forms of proper names and his spelling ("honour," "neighbour") are kept as he wrote them.

## Notes

The notes to the text are mine, not Long's. They explain names and historical allusions, identify quotations that Marcus copies without naming the author, and mark differences of reading. In Book I, where almost every passage names a person, the notes say briefly who each one was. The notes stand at the end of each chapter; in the electronic edition they can also be opened directly from the text.
