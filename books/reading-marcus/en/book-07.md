## Line of the Book

**Fanning the Coals.** The key to Book VII lies in its second passage: principles die if the impressions that correspond to them are not fanned into a flame (7.2). Almost the whole book is such fanning: short formulas, commands and extracts.

Sixteen key passages: nothing new (7.1) → how principles revive (7.2) → a bone cast to little dogs (7.3) → attend to words and acts (7.4) → reason does not disturb itself (7.16) → how many a Chrysippus time has swallowed (7.19) → to love those who do wrong (7.22) → a face distorted by anger (7.24) → seven commands (7.29) → on death (7.32) → Plato's view (7.35) → running with the stars (7.47) → all earthly things from above (7.48) → three things in man's constitution (7.55) → dig within (7.59) → a ready answer to pain (7.64).

## Introduction

Book VII has more passages than any other: seventy-five. Here are formulas for the chief threats, death, pain and fame (7.32–7.34); commands of a single phrase (7.29); and a whole section of extracts from other writers, from Plato, Antisthenes, Euripides and others (most of 7.35–7.51). These seem to be the very "selections from books" that Marcus Aurelius, as he says in [[03-14|3.14]], "wast reserving for thy old age."

The passages fall into several themes.

- **Principles at hand:** 7.1, 7.2, 7.29, 7.32, 7.33, 7.34, 7.64.
- **The ruling faculty and judgment:** 7.4, 7.14, 7.16, 7.17, 7.28, 7.30, 7.55, 7.59, 7.67, 7.68.
- **People and those who do wrong:** 7.22, 7.24, 7.26, 7.63, 7.65, 7.70, 7.71, 7.73.
- **The whole, flux and change:** 7.9, 7.10, 7.13, 7.18, 7.19, 7.23, 7.25, 7.47, 7.48, 7.75.
- **Fame and oblivion:** 7.6, 7.21, 7.34, 7.62.
- **Extracts from books:** most of 7.35–7.51.
- **Fate and the last day:** 7.54, 7.56, 7.57, 7.58, 7.69.

Among the passages without commentary, two deserve notice: 7.61, "The art of life is more like the wrestler's art than the dancer's," and 7.69, "The perfection of moral character consists in this, in passing every day as the last."

## 7.1

![[07-01#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

Book VII opens with a question: "What is badness?" The answer removes the fright: "It is that which thou hast often seen." Vice, then, is no novelty but the most worn-out story in the world.

Marcus tells himself to keep this thought ready, in Greek πρόχειρον ἔχε, for everything that happens: "keep this in mind, that it is that which thou hast often seen." An expected blow loses its force.

The same things fill "the old histories... those of the middle ages and those of our own day," and "cities and houses are filled" with them now. History invents no new events; it only changes the names. Hence the conclusion: "There is nothing new: all things are both familiar and short-lived." Since everything is familiar, there is nothing to wonder at; since everything is short-lived, there is nothing to fear and nothing to cling to.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^eternal-recurrence|Everything comes round again]]: there is nothing new in the world.

## 7.2

![[07-02#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

"How can our principles become dead, unless the impressions (thoughts) which correspond to them are extinguished?" Long's "become dead" renders νεκρωθῆναι exactly. A doctrine lives as long as the picture in which one imagines it lives. "Extinguished" is the Greek σβεσθῶσιν, as a fire goes out: an impression fades if one does not return to it. "But it is in thy power continuously to fan these thoughts into a flame"; the Greek ἀναζωπυρεῖν means to blow on coals until they glow again.

Then Marcus turns to himself: "I can have that opinion about anything, which I ought to have. If I can, why am I disturbed? The things which are external to my mind have no relation at all to my mind." This is one of the clearest formulas dividing what is in our power from what lies outside it.

The passage ends thus: "To recover thy life is in thy power. Look at things again as thou didst use to look at them; for in this consists the recovery of thy life." The Greek ἀναβιῶναι is stronger than "recover": it means "to come back to life." To come back to life here is to regain a lost way of seeing. It is the same thought as in [[05-09|5.9]]: practice asks not that you never stumble but that you return.

**Doctrines.** What is outside the mind has no relation to the mind, and [[app-d-map-of-practice#^dichotomy-of-control|judgment is always in our power]], so a principle that has gone out can always be fanned into flame again.

## 7.3

![[07-03#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

Human occupations are set here in a row with the scuffling of puppies and mice: "a bone cast to little dogs, a bit of bread into fish-ponds, labourings of ants and burden-carrying, runnings about of frightened little mice, puppets pulled by strings." This is the view from above, from whose height a grand procession and a fight over a bone look the same. The ants appeared already in [[05-01|5.1]], but there they were an example of creatures doing their work; here their "labourings" mean pointless strain. One and the same image serves different thoughts.

"It is thy duty then in the midst of such things to show good humour and not a proud air." The Greek is μὴ καταφρυαττόμενον, "not snorting," as a horse snorts and tosses its head; Long's "proud air" gives the sense but loses the image. The view from above gives no right to look down on others.

At the end comes a measure of a man's worth: "every man is worth just so much as the things are worth about which he busies himself." One who busies himself with show is worth no more than show; one who busies himself with virtue is worth virtue.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^virtue-is-sufficient|Virtue is sufficient for happiness]]: a man is worth exactly as much as what he busies himself with.

## 7.4

![[07-04#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

"In discourse thou must attend to what is said, and in every movement thou must observe what is doing." Neither another's words nor one's own impulse should escape attention. The Greek verb is παρακολουθεῖν, "to follow closely," from which comes the name of the attention whose practice closes Book VIII.

For each case there is a question: of an act one asks to what end it refers, of a word what it means. In Greek these are ἐπὶ τίνα σκοπόν, "toward what target," and τί τὸ σημαινόμενον, "what is signified." Long keeps the Greek order, "in the one... in the other," which leaves the reader to pair them. The word σημαινόμενον comes from Stoic logic, where it named the meaning, distinct both from the sound of the word and from the thing itself; Marcus carries it over into everyday practice.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^self-is-hegemonikon|The true self is reason]], and its work is to watch every word and every impulse.

## 7.16

![[07-16#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

Who really disturbs the soul? "The ruling faculty does not disturb itself; I mean, does not frighten itself or cause itself pain. But if any one else can frighten or pain it, let him do so." It will not bring itself into such a state.

Let the body take care of itself, "if it can," and "let it speak, if it suffers": the body has a voice, but only about its own affairs. The soul that is subject to fear and pain "will suffer nothing" unless it begins to judge what happens as a misfortune. In the Greek the soul here is ψυχάριον, "little soul," the same word Epictetus used of man in [[04-41|4.41]], where Long renders it "a little soul bearing about a corpse"; here he drops the diminutive.

Hence the conclusion: "The leading principle in itself wants nothing, unless it makes a want for itself; and therefore it is both free from perturbation and unimpeded, if it does not disturb and impede itself." Everything that can harm reason passes through its judgments. This continues [[04-03|4.3]]: things do not touch the soul.

**Doctrines.** Fear and pain are born in judgment, and [[app-d-map-of-practice#^dichotomy-of-control|judgment is in our power]].

## 7.19

![[07-19#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

All bodies are carried through the universal substance "as through a furious torrent"; in Greek χείμαρρος, the winter torrent, water swollen after the rains. Everything is swept away, and everything is at the same time united with the whole, "as the parts of our body with one another."

Then comes an ironic roll call of the dead: "How many a Chrysippus, how many a Socrates, how many an Epictetus has time already swallowed up!" Three summits of philosophy are named: Socrates, from whom the Stoics traced their lineage; Chrysippus, who built the Stoic system; Epictetus, whose discourses Marcus received from Rusticus ([[01-07|1.7]]). The plural is deliberate: "how many a Chrysippus" means that there were many like Chrysippus and time has swallowed them all. Even the greatest mind proves to be one of many.

Marcus tells himself to apply this thought "with reference to every man and thing."

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^unity-of-cosmos|All things are bound into one whole]], like the members of one body, and everything is carried off by the stream, even the best of men.

## 7.22

![[07-22#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

"It is peculiar to man to love even those who do wrong." The Greek is ἴδιον ἀνθρώπου, "a property of man," and Long's "peculiar to man" renders it exactly. This is not a feat of the sage but a mark of the human, and one who does not love those who err falls short not of the sage but of the man. "Those who do wrong" is in Greek τοὺς πταίοντας, "those who stumble": the error here is like a clumsy step, not a crime.

How is this to be achieved? Marcus gives four thoughts to keep ready: "that they are kinsmen, and that they do wrong through ignorance and unintentionally, and that soon both of you will die; and above all, that the wrong-doer has done thee no harm, for he has not made thy ruling faculty worse than it was before." The second thought is the simplest: no one chooses to err, and to be angry with one who errs is as strange as being angry with one who sees poorly.

Harm in the strict sense is one: the corruption of one's own soul, and no one can work it from outside. Insult, loss, obstruction remain in the world of externals, and it is easy to love the one who stumbles when he has nothing to take from you. The same thoughts sounded in [[02-01|2.1]]; here they are pressed into one sentence.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^no-harm-to-virtuous|No harm can be done to the virtuous]]. And [[app-d-map-of-practice#^oikeiosis|men are kin to one another]], so it is natural to man to love those who do wrong.

## 7.24

![[07-24#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

"A scowling look is altogether unnatural." Marcus does not reason about the harm of anger but looks at the face. When such an expression is assumed often, "the result is that all comeliness dies away, and at last is so completely extinguished that it cannot be again lighted up at all." The face remembers the passion, and one day the mask no longer comes off. Seneca opens his treatise *On Anger* with a similar portrait of a face disfigured by rage.

The words here are words of fire, as in [[07-02|7.2]]: comeliness is "extinguished," and it "cannot be again lighted up," which renders the Greek ἐξαφθῆναι exactly. Principles go out if they are not fanned; a human face goes out from the habit of anger.

"For if even the perception of doing wrong shall depart, what reason is there for living any longer?" As long as vice is felt as vice, a man is alive as a moral being. Worse than anger is only the state in which one does wrong without noticing it. This echoes [[03-01|3.1]], where reason failed before the body.

**Doctrines.** Anger is contrary to nature, and this can be seen even in the face; it is proper to man to [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|live according to nature]].

## 7.29

**Commentary.**

This is the densest passage in the book and perhaps a summary of almost all of Marcus's practice: behind each of the seven commands stands a whole exercise.

"Wipe out the imagination": the Greek ἐξάλειψον is used of wiping writing off wax. "Stop the pulling of the strings": the puppet again, στῆσον τὴν νευροσπαστίαν. "Confine thyself to the present": the Greek περίγραψον means "draw a line round" the present. "Understand well what happens either to thee or to another," that is, first see the event and only then judge it. "Divide and distribute every object into the causal [formal] and the material": this is the stripped view of [[06-13|6.13]]. "Think of thy last hour." And the last: "Let the wrong which is done by a man stay there where the wrong was done"; another's fault lives in another's soul, not in yours.

**Doctrines.** Imagination, impulse and judgment about others' faults are [[app-d-map-of-practice#^dichotomy-of-control|in our power]]; others' faults themselves are not.

## 7.32

![[07-32#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

Passages 7.32–7.34 are three short notes in a row with headings: on death, on pain, on fame. This is what Marcus in [[07-01|7.1]] tells himself to keep at hand: a folded answer to each chief threat.

The answer about death is again built as the dilemma "providence or atoms" of [[06-10|6.10]]. The Greek is precise: if the world is atoms, death is dispersion; if the world is a unity, death is "either extinction or change," ἤτοι σβέσις ἢ μετάστασις, either dissolution into the whole or passage into another state. Long blurs this structure by listing "a dispersion, or a resolution into atoms, or annihilation" side by side, but he keeps the conclusion exactly. There are no other outcomes, and none of them is terrible: dispersion and extinction leave no one to whom it could be bad, and change does not destroy. Whatever picture of the world one takes, there is nothing to fear.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^providence|The world is ordered by providence]], and death in it is no evil. And even if the world were made of atoms, there would still be nothing to fear.

## 7.35

![[07-35#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

With this passage begins a special part of Book VII, the extracts from other writers: Plato, Antisthenes, Euripides and others follow one after another almost to 7.51.

The first extract comes from Plato's *Republic* (486a), and in it Marcus finds the ground of his own exercise. "Takes a view of all time and of all substance" is in Greek θεωρία παντὸς μὲν χρόνου, πάσης δὲ οὐσίας, "the contemplation of all time and all substance," an exact definition of the view from above. The conclusion: to one for whom life does not seem great, death will not seem an evil either. The fear of death, then, grows with the overvaluing of life, and it is cured by scale.

**Doctrines.** One who sees life on the scale of all time will not think death terrible, and will accept it as he accepts all that happens: in this lies [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|acceptance of fate]].

## 7.47

![[07-47#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

One sentence holds two exercises. The first: "Look round at the courses of the stars, as if thou wert going along with them." The Greek συμπεριθέοντα means "running round with them": not looking at the stars from below, but circling with them in thought at a height where earthly affairs shrink. The second: "constantly consider the changes of the elements into one another," earth becoming water, water air, air fire, and back again.

What is this for? Such thoughts "purge away the filth of the terrene life"; the Greek is τὸν ῥύπον τοῦ χαμαὶ βίου, "the filth of life down on the ground." What works here is not argument but a change of the picture before one's eyes: in place of the bustle of the marketplace one sets before oneself the circling of the stars and the cycle of the elements, and pettiness leaves of itself.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^unity-of-cosmos|All things are bound into one whole]] and pass continually into one another.

## 7.48

![[07-48#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

The view from above again, this time in Plato's words: one should look at earthly things "as if he viewed them from some higher place." Then all human affairs are listed at once, from "assemblies, armies" and "marriages" to "lamentations, markets."

Marcus attributes these words to Plato, but they do not occur in the surviving dialogues; perhaps this is a paraphrase, perhaps a lost source. In spirit they are close to the extract from the *Republic* in [[07-35|7.35]].

The end of the list matters: "a mixture of all things and an orderly combination of contraries." Long renders the Greek συγκοσμούμενον exactly: not merely "put together" but "ordered together," from the same root as κόσμος, "order" and "world." From above the motley mixture turns out to be an order: births and deaths, feasts and funerals hold one another in place, like the opposites of Heraclitus. The view from above, then, not only diminishes human affairs but shows how they are joined.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^unity-of-cosmos|All things are bound into one whole]], even opposites.

**Commentary.**

The passage begins with a prohibition familiar from [[03-04|3.4]]: "Do not look around thee to discover other men's ruling principles," that is, do not guess what goes on in another's head. One should look straight "to what nature leads thee." Nature leads by two paths: "the universal nature through the things which happen to thee, and thy own nature through the acts which must be done by thee."

What, then, must a man do? "That which is according to its constitution," and Marcus names three points in order. "The prime principle then in man's constitution is the social": rational beings are made "for the sake of one another." The second is "not to yield to the persuasions of the body": reason must not give way to the motions of the senses and appetites, "for both are animal," while "the intelligent motion claims superiority." The third is "freedom from error and from deception," that is, not to assent hastily and not to let oneself be deceived.

"Let then the ruling principle, holding fast to these things, go straight on, and it has what is its own."

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^oikeiosis|Men are born for one another]]. [[app-d-map-of-practice#^self-is-hegemonikon|The true self is reason]], and it must have superiority over the body.

## 7.59

**Commentary.**

"Look within," Long writes, but the Greek is Ἔνδον σκάπτε, "dig within": not "look into yourself" or "listen to yourself," but dig, as a laborer digs. At the end of the passage Long too has the verb: "if thou wilt ever dig." The verb is chosen with care: this is labor, not contemplation.

The image changes the usual view. We usually look for where to get the good: impressions, recognition, things. The image suggests that one should not bring in the new but clear away. The fountain, πηγή, is already within. A man without external goods is not empty but buried: under the silt of others' opinions, valuations, fears and habits lies a pure spring, but one must dig down to it.

The condition is named too: the spring "will ever bubble up, if thou wilt ever dig." Long keeps the double ἀεί of the Greek, "ever... ever." The spring lives as long as it is kept open. It is the same thought as in [[07-02|7.2]]: principles go out if they are not fanned.

In [[04-03|4.3]] there was a retreat within, to which one can withdraw; here within is a fountain from which one can draw strength.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^self-is-hegemonikon|The true self is reason]], and the good is to be sought in it, not in externals.

## 7.64

![[07-64#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

Against pain Marcus has prepared an answer in three steps (compare [[07-33|7.33]]). The first: in every pain "let this thought be present, that there is no dishonour in it, nor does it make the governing intelligence worse." Pain damages neither the rationality in a man nor his capacity to live with others, so there is no real harm.

The second: "let this remark of Epicurus aid thee." Apart from this place, Epicurus is named only in [[09-41|9.41]]. The Epicureans were the Stoics' chief rivals, but Marcus takes the best from them without embarrassment: "pain is neither intolerable nor everlasting, if thou bearest in mind that it has its limits, and if thou addest nothing to it in imagination." "Addest... in imagination" renders προσδοξάζειν, "to opine in addition to what is there." Pain itself is limited; what makes it unbearable is what we add to it.

The third step widens the very notion of pain. Many disagreeable things "are the same as pain" though we do not notice it, "such as excessive drowsiness, and the being scorched by heat, and the having no appetite." Long renders ἀνορεκτεῖν exactly. Irritated by stuffiness or lack of sleep, we give way to the same enemy without calling it by name, and then one should say to oneself "that thou art yielding to pain."

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^no-harm-to-virtuous|No harm can be done to the virtuous]]: pain does not make the soul worse.

## The Book in Brief

**Key thought.** Book VII rests on the thought of its second passage: principles die out if the impressions that correspond to them are not fanned, and fanning them is always in our power ([[07-02|7.2]]). That is why it holds so many formulas at hand: seven commands ([[07-29|7.29]]), answers to death, pain and fame, extracts from Plato and the tragedians. The fountain of good is within, and it bubbles up as long as one keeps digging ([[07-59|7.59]]).

**Practice: the view from above.** Marcus attributes the formula to Plato: "he who is discoursing about men should look also at earthly things as if he viewed them from some higher place" ([[07-48|7.48]]). The exercise is to rise above the earth in thought and see human affairs all at once, like an anthill: assemblies, armies, marriages, courts, feasts and lamentations; then to see that in other ages life was the same ([[07-49|7.49]]); and finally to find oneself in this spectacle. From above, human affairs do not only shrink: Marcus sees in them "a mixture of all things and an orderly combination of contraries," the order of the whole, of which the one who looks remains a part.
