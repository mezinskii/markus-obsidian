This appendix is meant not as a manual of practice but as a guide to the *Meditations*: it leads to every passage where a doctrine or an exercise appears, not only to those discussed in the commentaries. For each doctrine and each exercise it says in a few words what it is, and lists the places where Marcus Aurelius returns to it. Passages with a commentary in this book are marked in bold; the rest can be read in the chapters in order. For each exercise the appendix notes which of the three disciplines it belongs to: the discipline of assent (not to assent to an impression until one has checked what in it comes from the thing and what from one's own valuation), of action (to act justly and for the good of others), or of desire (to accept what the course of nature brings). Pierre Hadot links them to the three parts of Stoic philosophy: assent to logic, action to ethics, desire to physics.

## Doctrines

### To distinguish what is in our power from what is not ^dichotomy-of-control
Some things depend on us: our judgments, impulses and desires. Others do not: the body, property, the opinion of others, the future, death itself. Good and evil lie only in the first, and so the second is no cause for distress.

### The true self is the ruling faculty ^self-is-hegemonikon
Of all that makes up a man, the self proper is only his reason, the ruling faculty. The body and the breath are given for a time and will return to nature, and everything else lies outside our power.

### Virtue is sufficient for happiness ^virtue-is-sufficient
For a life to be happy, only virtue is needed. Health, wealth, fame and even children are indifferent in the strict sense: it is natural to prefer them, but they do not make a man better or worse, and happiness does not depend on them.

### No harm can be done to the virtuous ^no-harm-to-virtuous
The only real evil is vice, and vice arises only from one's own assent to a bad judgment. So external things can wound the body and take away property, but they cannot corrupt a man against his will.

### Nothing external is our own ^externals-on-loan
The body, property, those close to us and life itself are given to us for a time and recalled in due season. A loss is the return of what was given for use.

### Fame is empty ^fame-is-empty
Praise, honor and posthumous memory are not goods: a thing does not become better by praise or worse without it. Still less is there reason to live for the opinion of people who will soon die or are not yet born.

### To live according to nature ^live-according-to-nature
The goal of life is to live in agreement with nature: with the nature of the whole and with one's own, which is rational and social. For man this means to live by reason.

### All things are bound into one whole ^unity-of-cosmos
The world is one living being, pervaded by one reason. All things in it are connected and pass into one another, and men share in this unity through a common mind.

### The world is ordered by providence ^providence
The world is governed by a reason that arranges everything for the good of the whole. Nothing happens by blind chance, even if the sense of what happens cannot be seen from the place of a single man. Marcus often tests this against the other possibility, a blind concourse of atoms, and comes to the same conclusion: in neither case is there anything to complain of.

### Acceptance of fate ^amor-fati
Everything that happens is a link in one chain of causes, that is, in the same rational order. So what happens is to be accepted and even loved, not met with the demand that the whole be arranged otherwise. This holds for death too: it is also one of the works of nature and comes in its season.

### Everything comes round again ^eternal-recurrence
Everything flows and changes, but in a circle: things dissolve and return into the reason of the whole, and the world passes through the same cycles. So there is nothing new in human affairs: the same scenes were played before and will be played again. Whoever has seen the present has seen everything.

### The world is a single city ^cosmopolis
Gods and rational men make up one city, and the reason common to them serves as its law. Man is first a citizen of this city, and only then of his own.

### Men are born for one another ^oikeiosis
A rational being by nature reaches out to its kind: care for oneself widens into care for family, city and all men. Men are akin to one another through reason and are made for a common work. The same circle of kinship can be widened to the whole of nature: whoever has made nature his own recognizes his own in all her works.

### Gratitude to the gods ^gratitude-to-gods
If the world is ordered by providence, then life, reason and circumstances are received as gifts, and the natural answer to them is gratitude. This is not politeness or a request for more, but an acknowledgment of what has been given.

## Exercises

### Keeping at hand ^prokheiron
To reduce doctrines to short formulas and keep them ready, so as to apply them at once, without deliberating, as a fighter uses his hands. The discipline of assent.

### The stripped view ^definitio
To describe a thing or an event as it is, without valuation or ornament: what it is made of, what it will dissolve into. Then what opinion has added to the thing falls away. The discipline of assent.

### Attending to the movements of the soul ^parakolouthesis
To watch soberly what impressions arrive, what one assents to and what impulses arise from it, and above all to notice what the mind has added of its own to the first impression. The aim is to catch a false assent at once, not by its consequences. The discipline of assent.

### Retiring into oneself ^inner-retreat
To withdraw not to the country or the sea but into one's own ruling faculty, where it is always quiet. Such a retreat is available at any hour and is needed in order to return to one's work renewed. The discipline of assent.

### Notes to oneself ^hypomnemata
To write down for oneself principles already known, so as to repeat them again and again and keep them ready. This is neither a diary nor a treatise: such notes have no reader but the one who writes them. The discipline of assent.

### Attention ^prosokhe
To be wholly present in what one is doing now, and at the same time to remember for what purpose and by what rule it is done. To do each act as if it were the last. The discipline of action.

### No more delay ^no-more-delay
Not to wait until affairs are settled: philosophy begins now, because there has been delay enough and little time remains. The discipline of action.

### The morning meditation ^morning-meditation
Before meeting people and business, to prepare oneself in advance: to remind oneself what one will meet in the day and what one rises for. The discipline of action.

### The remembrance of death ^meditatio-mortis
To keep death in mind as a near possibility and to treat the body "as if thou wast now dying" ([[02-02|2.2]]). This is not fear but a way of concentrating on what matters now and discarding the superfluous. The discipline of desire.

### The view from above ^view-from-above
To rise in thought above the earth and look at human affairs from a height. Passions and cares become small, and one's own belonging to the whole is seen more clearly. The discipline of desire.

### The contemplation of nature ^contemplation-of-nature
To look closely at any natural process, even withering and decay, and to see its place and necessity in the common order. So aversion gives way to understanding and acceptance. The discipline of desire.

### The anticipation of evils ^praemeditatio
To picture in advance the troubles that may come: meeting bad people, illness, loss. A trouble seen beforehand loses its suddenness and does not take one by surprise. The discipline of desire.
