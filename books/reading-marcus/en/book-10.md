## The Thread of the Book

**Time to Become One.** Book X opens with a question Marcus Aurelius puts to his own soul (10.1), and almost all of it says the same thing: stop discussing what a good man should be and be one (10.16), give yourself not rules but names (10.8), turn every circumstance into material for the work (10.33).

Eight key passages: perishing as change (10.7) → names instead of rules (10.8) → method and magnanimity (10.11) → a morning question about praise (10.13) → time to be such (10.16) → everything as it were dies (10.18) → where are they all (10.31) → what to make of the material (10.33).

## Introduction

Marcus Aurelius asks his own soul: "Wilt thou, then, my soul, never be good and simple and one and naked, more manifest than the body which surrounds thee?" (10.1). This is the question of a man who has long known what he ought to be and sees that he has not yet become it.

Here too he explains why it is so hard. "Mimi, war, astonishment, torpor, slavery, will daily wipe out those holy principles of thine" (10.9). Long's "Mimi" is the Greek μῖμος, the mime, the low farce of the Roman stage; the word seems to stand for the whole spectacle of public life. The Greek adds which principles are wiped out: those that one holds ἀφυσιολογήτως, "without studying nature." This continues [[07-02|7.2]]: principles die out if they are not fanned into a flame, and those not thought through to the end are put out by life itself, and daily.

The passages fall into several themes.

- **Time to become one:** 10.1, 10.8, 10.16, 10.24, 10.32.
- **The whole and the acceptance of one's lot:** 10.5, 10.6, 10.7, 10.11, 10.20, 10.21, 10.25, 10.26, 10.28.
- **Flux, death, oblivion:** 10.15, 10.17, 10.18, 10.27, 10.29, 10.31, 10.34, 10.36.
- **Others' judgments and faults:** 10.4, 10.13, 10.19, 10.30, 10.37.
- **Reason and the material of action:** 10.2, 10.3, 10.9, 10.12, 10.33, 10.35, 10.38.

Among the passages without commentary, note 10.15: "Short is the little which remains to thee of life. Live as on a mountain." And further: "Let men see, let them know a real man who lives according to nature."

## 10.7

![[10-07#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

"The parts of the whole... must of necessity perish; but let this be understood in this sense, that they must undergo change." Marcus replaces the word at once: let perishing be called change, in Greek ἀλλοιοῦσθαι, "to become other." From here on the whole passage speaks of change, and fear loses its object.

If change were an evil for the parts, then either nature designed "to do evil to the things which are parts of herself," or such results "happened without her knowing it." "Both these suppositions, indeed, are incredible": the first contradicts her goodness, the second her intelligence. It follows that change is not an evil.

Marcus makes a concession even to one who does not believe in a rational nature. Let a man "drop the term Nature" and speak of things simply as natural; even then "it would be ridiculous" to hold that the parts are by nature subject to change and at the same time to be vexed "as if something were happening contrary to nature." Indignation contradicts its own premise.

Dissolution is not annihilation either: a thing breaks up into what it was made of, and its parts "are taken back into the universal reason."

The passage ends thus: "do not imagine that the solid and the airy part belong to thee from the time of generation. For all this received its accretion only yesterday and the day before, as one may say, from the food and the air which is inspired." The body we fear for is not the one we were born with: it has been gathered from food and air over the last few days and is constantly renewed. Change goes on in us all the time, and death only continues it. In [[06-15|6.15]] the same is said of breathing: the respiratory power "which thou didst receive at thy birth yesterday and the day before" must be given back "to the element from which thou didst first draw it."

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^unity-of-cosmos|All things are bound into one whole]], and its parts change. [[app-d-map-of-practice#^providence|The world is ordered by providence]], and change is not an evil. [[app-d-map-of-practice#^eternal-recurrence|Everything in the end returns into the reason of the whole]].

## 10.8

![[10-08#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

Marcus gives himself six names: "good, modest, true, rational, a man of equanimity, and magnanimous." A rule says what to do; a name says who you are. That is why he sees the danger not in breaking a rule but in changing the names, that is, in becoming someone else. The names can be lost, and Marcus allows for this; then one thing is needed: "quickly return to them."

He explains the last three names at once. In Greek they sound almost alike: ἔμφρων, σύμφρων, ὑπέρφρων. The root is the same, φρήν, "mind," and only the prefixes differ. The ἔμφρων, "rational," gives "a discriminating attention to every several thing." The σύμφρων, literally "of one mind with," makes "the voluntary acceptance of the things which are assigned to thee by the common nature"; Long calls him "a man of equanimity." The ὑπέρφρων, literally "minding from above," keeps the intelligent part "above the pleasurable or painful sensations of the flesh, and above that poor thing called fame, and death." Long conveys the sense; the play on the one root is lost.

These names are to be kept "without desiring to be called by these names by others": a name one waits for others to bestow turns into fame.

To stay as one has been is to be like "those half-devoured fighters with wild beasts, who though covered with wounds and gore, still intreat to be kept to the following day." Long is exact here: the Greek θηριομάχοι are fighters with beasts and ἡμίβρωτοι "half-eaten." They beg for one more day, though tomorrow they will be exposed "to the same claws and bites."

Then three outcomes are named. If you can abide in the names, abide "as if thou wast removed to certain islands of the Happy." If you feel yourself falling out of them, "go courageously into some nook where thou shalt maintain them." And if even that fails, "depart at once from life, not in passion, but with simplicity and freedom and modesty," as in [[05-29|5.29]]. The names are easier to remember, Marcus adds, if one remembers the gods, and that "what does the work of a fig-tree is a fig-tree, and that what does the work of a dog is a dog, and that what does the work of a bee is a bee, and that what does the work of a man is a man."

**Doctrines.** As a fig tree does the work of a fig tree, so a man does the work of a man: this is to [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|live according to nature]]. The man of "equanimity" accepts his lot voluntarily, and in this lies [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|acceptance of fate]]. And [[app-d-map-of-practice#^fame-is-empty|fame is empty]]: the names are not worn so that others may pronounce them.

## 10.11

![[10-11#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

"Acquire the contemplative way of seeing how all things change into one another, and constantly attend to it, and exercise thyself about this part of philosophy. For nothing is so much adapted to produce magnanimity." The last word in Greek is μεγαλοφροσύνη, greatness of mind. The study of nature (for the Stoics, "physics") is here not a theory but an exercise: the habit of seeing how everything passes into everything else widens one's view.

Marcus then describes the man who has mastered this exercise: "Such a man has put off the body." The Greek ἐξεδύσατο τὸ σῶμα means the body has been taken off like a garment. He remembers that he will soon leave all this behind, and "he gives himself up entirely to just doing in all his actions, and in everything else that happens he resigns himself to the universal nature." The division is exact: do one's own part justly, entrust the rest to the whole.

"But as to what any man shall say or think about him or do against him, he never even thinks of it." He is content with two things: "acting justly in what he now does, and being satisfied with what is now assigned to him." Here Long is too mild: the Greek says φιλεῖ, he "loves" what is assigned to him, which is more than being satisfied with it. At the end comes the image of a road: he desires nothing else "than to accomplish the straight course through the law, and by accomplishing the straight course to follow God." The Greek εὐθεῖαν περαίνειν means "to go the straight way."

**Doctrines.** Do one's own part justly and entrust the rest to the nature of the whole: this is where the line runs between [[app-d-map-of-practice#^dichotomy-of-control|what is in our power and what is not]]. Such a man does not merely endure his lot but loves it, and this is [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|acceptance of fate]]. And to go the straight way after the law is to [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|live according to nature]].

## 10.13

![[10-13#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

This is another morning exercise, and its time is set precisely: "as soon as thou wakest from sleep." In Long the question runs: "whether it will make any difference to thee, if another does what is just and right." The Greek in Leopold's text says something else: ἐὰν ὑπὸ ἄλλου ψέγηται, "if what is just and right is blamed by another." Long apparently translated from an edition with a different word at this point: the text of the *Meditations* survives in very few manuscripts, and editors sometimes read it differently. Leopold's reading fits better with what follows, which is about those who hand out praise and blame.

"Thou hast not forgotten, I suppose, that those who assume arrogant airs in bestowing their praise or blame on others, are such as they are at bed and at board." The device is not to argue with the judgment but to look at the one who judges: the value of praise falls with the value of the one who praises.

A strong sentence follows: these people steal and rob "not with hands and feet, but with their most valuable part": vice is committed not by the body but by the mind. That same part is the one "by means of which there is produced, when a man chooses, fidelity, modesty, truth, law, a good daemon." The Greek is stronger still: that part itself "becomes, when it wishes," fidelity, modesty, truth, law, a good daemon. Long adds in brackets "(happiness)," and rightly: from the last words, ἀγαθὸς δαίμων, comes the Greek word for happiness, εὐδαιμονία, literally "having a good daemon." So the morning question about others' praise ends with a description of happiness, and it has one condition: to will it.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^fame-is-empty|Fame is empty]]: praise and blame are worth no more than those who hand them out.

## 10.16

![[10-16#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

This is one of the shortest and sharpest passages of the *Meditations*: Marcus reproaches himself for discussing the good man instead of becoming one.

Long keeps the force of the Greek: "No longer talk at all." The adverb ὅλως, "at all," leaves no loophole even for a little discussion. And the verb is chosen with care: διαλέγεσθαι means not simply "to talk" but to reason, to hold a discussion, and it was also the word for philosophical conversation. Not διαλέγεσθαι but εἶναι: not to discuss, but to be.

Why so sharp? Because talk about virtue is comfortable: it is endless and binds one to nothing. As long as "the good man" is being discussed, he remains somewhere ahead, beyond one more book and one more conversation.

To read about philosophy, to hear about philosophy, to write about philosophy is not yet to practice it. Seneca says the same: "philosophy teaches us to act, not to speak" (Letters 20.2, trans. Gummere).

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^virtue-is-sufficient|Virtue is sufficient for happiness]], and one can become virtuous only now, not in a conversation about virtue.

## 10.18

**Commentary.**

To see every thing as "already in dissolution": such is this brief exercise of the imagination.

Usually imagination adds luster to a thing; this exercise shows its end. The key word in Greek is ἤδη, "already," and Long keeps it: to see a thing not as it is now but as it will inevitably become. The stripped view of [[06-13|6.13]], where Falernian wine turns out to be the juice of grapes, is here extended in time.

There are several ways of passing away: dissolution, change, putrefaction, dispersion. And the last: "everything is so constituted by nature as to die." The Greek is more careful: καθότι ἕκαστον πέφυκεν ὥσπερ θνῄσκειν, "as each thing is by nature formed, as it were, to die." Long keeps the "as it were" before putrefaction but drops it here, where it matters most. The manner of each thing's end, then, is set by nature from the start. Not only living things die: a stone, a cloth, a city also die in their own way. Death ceases to be a special event and becomes the common name for what happens to every thing.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^unity-of-cosmos|All things are bound into one whole]], and each of its parts changes and in its own way dies.

**Commentary.**

At first sight this is a puzzling list of names. "When thou hast seen Satyron the Socratic, think of either Eutyches or Hymen, and when thou hast seen Euphrates, think of Eutychion or Silvanus." Most of these people are unknown today, but the device is clear without them: in each pair, it seems, a man Marcus has before him is set beside a long-dead man of the same type. Looking at one, picture the one who was the same before him. Among the names is Severus, perhaps the one Marcus thanks in [[01-14|1.14]]. Long reverses this pair; the Greek says literally, "when thou hast seen Severus, think of Crito or Xenophon."

The last pair matters most: "when thou hast looked on thyself, think of any other Caesar." Marcus counts himself among them, and he does not even name the Caesar beside him: not Augustus, not Hadrian, but "any."

The question follows naturally: "Where then are those men?" And the answer: "Nowhere, or nobody knows where." Marcus does not choose between annihilation and another life: for the argument it is enough that they are not here. So one will see "human things as smoke and nothing at all."

The second half of the passage, however, turns the thought. "What matter and opportunity for thy activity art thou avoiding? For what else are all these things, except exercises for the reason." What seemed a hindrance becomes raw material. Two images are given as well: as "the stomach which is strengthened makes all things its own," as "the blazing fire makes flame and brightness out of everything that is thrown into it."

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^fame-is-empty|Fame is empty]]: where are they all? And everything that happens can become material for reason.

## 10.33

![[10-33#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

"What is that which as to this material (our life) can be done or said in the way most conformable to reason. For whatever this may be, it is in thy power to do it or to say it, and do not make excuses that thou art hindered." Circumstances are called here "material," in Greek ὕλη, the raw stuff of work.

Marcus then borrows a comparison from people whose way of life he does not approve of. For those who enjoy pleasure there is "luxury," and in it lies their delight. A man will not cease to lament, he says, until the doing of what accords with his constitution is to him what luxury is to them, whatever the material given him. "For a man ought to consider as an enjoyment everything which it is in his power to do according to his own nature. And it is in his power everywhere."

The central comparison is this. To a cylinder, to water, to fire their own motion is not always given: "the things which check them and stand in the way are many." But "intelligence and reason are able to go through everything that opposes them." And at once the image turns: reason is carried through all things "as fire upwards, as a stone downwards, as a cylinder down an inclined surface." A moment ago the cylinder was an example of limitation; now it is an image of the unstoppable.

Why does an obstacle do no harm? If it did, "he who felt it would immediately become bad." With a man the reverse happens: he becomes "both better, if one may say so, and more worthy of praise by making a right use of these accidents."

The last argument is taken from the life of a city: "nothing harms him who is really a citizen, which does not harm the state; nor yet does anything harm the state, which does not harm law (order)." The state here is the whole world, and its law is its rational order. Misfortunes do not harm that order, and so they harm neither the world nor man as its citizen.

**Doctrines.** The material is given from outside, but [[app-d-map-of-practice#^dichotomy-of-control|what to make of it is always in our power]]. [[app-d-map-of-practice#^no-harm-to-virtuous|No harm can be done to the virtuous]]. And [[app-d-map-of-practice#^cosmopolis|the world is a single city]], in which only what harms the law does harm.

## The Book in Brief

**Key thought.** Marcus has long known what he ought to be, and Book X is about the distance between knowledge and life. Reasoning will not shorten it ([[10-16|10.16]]). It can be shortened otherwise: by holding to a few names and returning to them when they are lost ([[10-08|10.8]]), and by asking in any situation what it would be most right to do now ([[10-33|10.33]]).

**Practice: the remembrance of death (meditatio mortis).** In Book X Marcus returns to it many times: to see every thing as "already in dissolution" ([[10-18|10.18]]), to ask of the great men of the past "Where then are those men?" ([[10-31|10.31]]), to remember that "short is the little which remains to thee of life" ([[10-15|10.15]]). He keeps the most personal argument for his own last hour: even those close to him may breathe more freely when he is gone, and say, "Let us at last breathe freely, being relieved from this schoolmaster" ([[10-36|10.36]]). And at once comes the qualification: "Do not, however, for this reason go away less kindly disposed to them."
