This glossary is for the reader who has met a Greek word in a commentary and wants to recall quickly what it means. It gathers the chief terms of the *Meditations* in the order of the Greek alphabet; the article is ignored in sorting. Each entry gives the word in transliteration, a short translation, and a few sentences on what it means for the Stoics and how Marcus Aurelius uses it. At the end of the entry the main places are listed, no more than eight: first the passages discussed in this book, then others where the term occurs. Fuller entries with Greek examples and sources are at readaurelius.org.

The English equivalents given here are approximate: one Greek word often has to be rendered by different English words, and Long himself does not always translate it the same way.

### ἀγαθόν

*agathon*: the good.

For the Stoics there is only one good: virtue and what shares in it; health, wealth and fame do not belong to it. The good is inseparable from the beautiful (καλόν), and evil from the shameful. Already in [[02-01|2.1]] the knowledge of what good and evil are frees Marcus from anger at people: none of them can make him bad. Main places: [[02-01|2.1]], [[03-06|3.6]], [[04-17|4.17]], [[05-16|5.16]], [[06-30|6.30]], [[07-59|7.59]], [[08-01|8.1]], [[08-05|8.5]] and elsewhere.

### ἄγνοια

*agnoia*: ignorance.

For the Stoics the root of vice: a man acts badly not from ill will but because he is mistaken about what is good and evil. Hence Marcus's chief move toward those who wrong him: not anger, but the understanding that the man is in error and harms himself first of all ([[02-01|2.1]], [[07-22|7.22]], [[11-18|11.18]]). Main places: [[02-01|2.1]], [[02-13|2.13]], [[05-18|5.18]], [[06-21|6.21]], [[06-22|6.22]], [[07-22|7.22]], [[07-66|7.66]], [[11-18|11.18]].

### ἀδιάφορα

*adiaphora* (plural of ἀδιάφορον): indifferent things.

Everything that is neither virtue nor vice: life and death, fame and obscurity, pain and pleasure, wealth and poverty. Indifferent does not mean worthless: among indifferent things some are preferred and some dispreferred (see ἀξία), but neither makes a life succeed or fail. Marcus draws this from a simple observation: such things fall alike to good men and bad ([[02-11|2.11]]). Main places: [[05-20|5.20]], [[06-32|6.32]], [[07-31|7.31]], [[08-56|8.56]], [[11-16|11.16]].

### αἰδώς

*aidōs*: reverence, a sense of shame, self-respect.

The word joins shame before others, reverence and respect for oneself. For the Stoics it is above all respect for one's own rational nature, which must not be degraded. In [[02-06|2.6]] Marcus reproaches his soul for lacking just this respect: "thy soul reverences not itself but places thy felicity in the souls of others." Main places: [[01-02|1.2]], [[02-06|2.6]], [[02-13|2.13]], [[06-16|6.16]], [[06-30|6.30]], [[10-08|10.8]], [[10-13|10.13]], [[11-01|11.1]] and elsewhere.

### ἁμαρτία

*hamartia*: a fault, an error; literally "a miss."

Originally an archer's miss of the target (see σκοπός). The Stoics distinguish a single fault (ἁμάρτημα) from vice as a settled state (κακία). Marcus thinks a great deal about the faults of others: the one who errs has missed through ignorance; it is his affair, not yours, and his miss should be no occasion for anger. Main places: [[02-10|2.10]], [[02-11|2.11]], [[04-03|4.3]], [[07-22|7.22]], [[07-24|7.24]], [[07-29|7.29]], [[09-42|9.42]], [[11-18|11.18]] and elsewhere.

### ἀνδρεία

*andreia*: courage, fortitude.

One of the four cardinal virtues. For the Stoics courage is the knowledge of what is truly to be feared (only vice) and what is not (death, pain, loss). In [[05-12|5.12]] fortitude stands in the list of true goods, and in [[11-18|11.18]] Marcus gives strength and courage to the gentle man rather than the angry one. Main places: [[05-12|5.12]], [[11-18|11.18]].

### ἀντιπράσσειν

*antiprassein*: to act against.

To act against others, to hinder the common work. Rational beings are made for co-operation (see συνεργία), so acting against one another is contrary to nature. With this thought [[02-01|2.1]] ends: "it is acting against one another to be vexed and to turn away." Main places: [[02-01|2.1]].

### ἀξία

*axia*: value, worth.

For the Stoics the word for the weight of indifferent things: health has value, and illness the opposite (ἀπαξία), but neither is good or evil. This allows one to act reasonably in the world: to seek a cure, to work, to care for those close to one, without making one's good depend on the outcome. Marcus is glad when his ruling faculty uses each thing according to its worth ([[08-43|8.43]]). Main places: [[03-11|3.11]], [[05-30|5.30]], [[05-36|5.36]], [[06-03|6.3]], [[07-03|7.3]], [[08-29|8.29]], [[11-37|11.37]], [[12-01|12.1]] and elsewhere.

### ἀπάθεια

*apatheia*: freedom from passion.

Freedom from the passions, that is, from false judgments about good and evil, not insensibility. The sage feels pain and knows how to love, but does not assent to the thought that an evil has befallen him. The English "apathy," dull indifference, means something quite different. Marcus recalls how Sextus joined complete freedom from passion with the greatest affection for people ([[01-09|1.9]]). Main places: [[01-09|1.9]], [[06-16|6.16]], [[11-18|11.18]].

### ἀπόρροια

*aporroia*: efflux, emanation.

A stream that has flowed out from its source. Marcus seems to take the word from the common philosophical language; for the same thought the Stoics more often said "fragment" (ἀπόσπασμα). The image shows that there is no break between the source and the stream. In [[02-04|2.4]] Marcus asks himself "of what administrator of the universe thy existence is an efflux." A related image: θεία ἀπόμοιρα, the divine portion. Main places: [[02-04|2.4]].

### ἀρετή

*aretē*: virtue, excellence.

The excellence of a thing according to its nature: in a knife, sharpness; in a man, virtue. For the Stoics virtue is the only good, a state of the ruling faculty in agreement with reason; its four chief forms are wisdom, justice, courage and temperance. To delight himself, Marcus advises thinking of "the virtues of those who live with thee" ([[06-48|6.48]]). Main places: [[02-13|2.13]], [[03-07|3.7]], [[03-11|3.11]], [[06-17|6.17]], [[06-48|6.48]], [[07-31|7.31]], [[07-68|7.68]], [[09-42|9.42]] and elsewhere.

### γαλήνη

*galēnē*: calm, tranquility.

The stillness of a windless sea. Marcus uses it for the calm of the soul. In [[05-02|5.2]] he notes how easy it is to wipe away every troublesome impression "and immediately to be in all tranquility." Main places: [[05-02|5.2]], [[07-28|7.28]], [[07-33|7.33]], [[07-68|7.68]], [[08-28|8.28]], [[12-22|12.22]].

### δαίμων

*daimōn*: daemon, the divine principle in the soul.

In Plato and the Stoics the highest, rational part of the soul, "the god within us"; Epictetus taught that Zeus gave each man his daemon as a guardian. In Marcus the daemon is almost the same as the ruling faculty and the mind. To serve it is to keep it pure from passion, from thoughtlessness and from dissatisfaction with what comes from gods and men ([[02-13|2.13]]); in [[05-27|5.27]] the daemon is called outright "every man's understanding and reason." Main places: [[02-13|2.13]], [[02-17|2.17]], [[03-03|3.3]], [[03-06|3.6]], [[03-07|3.7]], [[05-27|5.27]], [[10-13|10.13]], [[12-03|12.3]] and elsewhere.

### δικαιοσύνη

*dikaiosynē*: justice.

One of the four cardinal virtues: the knowledge of how to give each his due. It is the virtue turned toward people, and for Marcus, who sees man as a social being, it stands in the foreground. He often says δικαιότης; in [[02-05|2.5]] justice closes the list of what every act should be done with, and in [[11-01|11.1]] "the right reason differs not at all from the reason of justice." Main places: [[02-05|2.5]], [[03-04|3.4]], [[03-06|3.6]], [[04-03|4.3]], [[10-11|10.11]], [[11-01|11.1]], [[12-01|12.1]], [[12-03|12.3]] and elsewhere.

### εἱμαρμένη

*heimarmenē*: fate, destiny.

The connected chain of causes running through the whole world. For the Stoics this is not the blind necessity of tragedy but a rational order, the same as the nature of the whole and providence. Hence Marcus's advice not to be dissatisfied with one's present lot or to shrink from the future ([[02-02|2.2]]): whatever happens to you was destined for you from the beginning together with the whole ([[04-26|4.26]]). Main places: [[02-02|2.2]], [[03-06|3.6]], [[04-26|4.26]], [[05-08|5.8]], [[05-24|5.24]], [[07-46|7.46]], [[08-35|8.35]], [[12-03|12.3]] and elsewhere.

### ἐπιθυμία

*epithymia*: desire, appetite.

One of the four chief passions: the false judgment that some external good must be obtained. The Stoics counted anger too as a kind of desire, a craving for revenge. In [[02-10|2.10]] Marcus, following Theophrastus, writes that "the offences which are committed through desire are more blameable than those which are committed through anger": the angry man acts with pain, the one who desires with pleasure. Not to be confused with ὁρμή. Main places: [[02-10|2.10]], [[03-07|3.7]], [[07-16|7.16]], [[08-29|8.29]], [[09-40|9.40]], [[10-01|10.1]], [[12-19|12.19]].

### εὐδαιμονία

*eudaimonia*: happiness.

A life that has succeeded as a whole; literally "having a good daemon." For the Stoics virtue alone leads to happiness, and happiness itself is "a good flow of life" (see εὔροια). Marcus also says εὐμοιρία, "a good lot": in [[02-06|2.6]] the soul errs in placing it "in the souls of others," and in [[07-17|7.17]] the word εὐδαιμονία itself is unfolded: "Eudaemonia [happiness] is a good daemon, or a good thing." Main places: [[07-17|7.17]], [[10-13|10.13]].

### εὔροια

*euroia*: a good flow of life.

So the Stoics, beginning with Zeno, defined happiness: a life in which reason, desire and action flow in one direction, like a river without rapids. This means not a pleasant or a successful life but a life in agreement with itself. Marcus himself says εὔρους βίος, "a life that flows well," and εὐροεῖν, "to flow well." In [[02-05|2.5]] ("a life which flows in quiet," in Long's words) he notes how little is needed to live so; in [[05-09|5.9]] Long renders the same word "the happy course." Main places: [[02-05|2.5]], [[05-09|5.9]], [[05-34|5.34]], [[10-06|10.6]].

### ἡγεμονικόν

*hēgemonikon*: the ruling faculty.

The highest, rational part of the soul: it forms judgments, gives assent to impressions and produces impulses. External things can touch the body but not the ruling faculty, unless it consents itself; in this the Stoics saw man's freedom. Long renders it variously as "the ruling faculty," "the ruling part," "the ruling principle" and "the leading principle." Marcus divides himself into flesh, breath and the ruling part ([[02-02|2.2]]) and calls the mind free from passions a citadel, ἀκρόπολις ([[08-48|8.48]]). Main places: [[02-02|2.2]], [[03-04|3.4]], [[06-36|6.36]], [[07-16|7.16]], [[07-22|7.22]], [[07-55|7.55]], [[08-03|8.3]], [[08-56|8.56]] and elsewhere.

### ἡδονή

*hēdonē*: pleasure.

One of the four chief passions: the false judgment that what is being experienced now is a good. The Stoics distinguished it from rational joy (χαρά). In Marcus pleasure stands in a row with pain, fame and death among the things that make a man neither better nor worse ([[02-11|2.11]]), and in [[05-09|5.9]] he notes that pleasure leads us astray precisely by being agreeable. Main places: [[02-17|2.17]], [[03-03|3.3]], [[03-04|3.4]], [[03-06|3.6]], [[04-03|4.3]], [[05-09|5.9]], [[09-01|9.1]], [[12-08|12.8]] and elsewhere.

### θεία ἀπόμοιρα

*theia apomoira*: the divine portion.

A part of the divine reason of the world allotted to each man; the Stoics also said "a fragment of God" (ἀπόσπασμα θεοῦ). On this Marcus builds the kinship of men: each "participates in the same intelligence and the same portion of the divinity" ([[02-01|2.1]]). In [[12-26|12.26]] he reminds himself that "every man's intelligence is a god and is an efflux of the Deity." Main places: [[02-01|2.1]], [[05-27|5.27]], [[12-01|12.1]], [[12-26|12.26]].

### θυμός

*thymos*: anger, passion.

In Plato the "spirited" part of the soul. The Stoics held the soul to be one, and anger a kind of desire, a craving for revenge. In [[02-10|2.10]] Marcus, following Theophrastus, judges offenses from anger lighter than offenses from desire; in [[11-18|11.18]] he examines at length how not to be angry and calls gentleness more manly than rage. Main places: [[02-10|2.10]], [[07-38|7.38]], [[11-18|11.18]].

### καθῆκον

*kathēkon*: appropriate action; often translated "duty."

What it is reasonable to do in one's position: toward family, friends, city. It is not yet the perfect act of the sage (κατόρθωμα), but the right content of an act, open also to one who is only making progress. In [[01-12|1.12]] Marcus recalls the lesson of Alexander the Platonist: not to plead lack of leisure in order to avoid duties toward those with whom one lives; in [[03-01|3.1]] he fears that with age the power of "filling up the measure of our duty" may weaken. Main places: [[01-12|1.12]], [[03-01|3.1]], [[03-16|3.16]], [[06-22|6.22]], [[06-26|6.26]], [[07-05|7.5]], [[10-03|10.3]], [[12-17|12.17]].

### κακία

*kakia*: vice, badness.

A settled state of the soul contrary to reason, the opposite of virtue; a single act is called otherwise (see ἁμαρτία). For the Stoics only vice is real evil. Marcus reminds himself that there is nothing new in vice: "What is badness? It is that which thou hast often seen" ([[07-01|7.1]]). Main places: [[04-03|4.3]], [[05-35|5.35]], [[06-01|6.1]], [[07-01|7.1]], [[07-31|7.31]], [[07-66|7.66]], [[08-56|8.56]], [[12-01|12.1]] and elsewhere.

### κακοδαιμονία

*kakodaimonia*: unhappiness.

Literally "having a bad daemon"; the opposite of εὐδαιμονία. For the Stoics this is not a misfortune coming from outside but a state of the soul itself, which seeks its good in externals. In [[02-08|2.8]] Marcus writes that "those who do not observe the movements of their own minds must of necessity be unhappy." Main places: [[02-08|2.8]].

### κακόν

*kakon*: evil.

For the Stoics the only real evil is vice and what shares in it; illness, death and disgrace are not evils. Hence Marcus's chief argument: another man cannot make me bad, and so cannot do me evil ([[02-01|2.1]]). What happens alike to good men and bad is neither evil nor good ([[02-11|2.11]]). Main places: [[02-01|2.1]], [[02-17|2.17]], [[08-01|8.1]], [[08-28|8.28]], [[08-56|8.56]], [[09-42|9.42]], [[10-07|10.7]], [[10-33|10.33]] and elsewhere.

### κατόρθωμα

*katorthōma*: right action, a perfect act.

An appropriate act done from settled virtue, that is, the act of the sage. The word is connected with ὀρθός, "straight." In [[05-14|5.14]] (where Marcus uses κατόρθωσις, a word of the same root) he explains that "such acts are named Catorthoseis or right acts, which word signifies that they proceed by the right road." Main places: [[05-14|5.14]].

### κοινωνικόν

*koinōnikon*: the social principle.

The property of rational nature by which a man lives not for himself but for the whole. For Marcus to be rational and to be social are one and the same, and the work of a human being consists of social acts. In the morning admonition of [[05-01|5.1]] Marcus reproaches himself for valuing "the acts which concern society" less than the turner values his art, and in [[05-16|5.16]] "the good for the reasonable animal is society." Main places: [[05-01|5.1]], [[05-16|5.16]], [[05-29|5.29]], [[06-30|6.30]], [[07-55|7.55]], [[07-64|7.64]], [[08-07|8.7]], [[08-12|8.12]] and elsewhere.

### λόγος

*logos*: reason, word, the reason of the world.

For the Stoics the reason of the world, the same as god, nature and fate, pervades everything; man's reason is a part of it and therefore common to all men. From this Marcus concludes that we have a common law and that the world is like a city ([[04-04|4.4]]). The word can also mean simply "speech" or "word" ([[04-33|4.33]]). Main places: [[02-05|2.5]], [[03-06|3.6]], [[04-05|4.5]], [[04-14|4.14]], [[04-33|4.33]], [[05-09|5.9]], [[05-27|5.27]], [[07-24|7.24]] and elsewhere.

### λύπη

*lypē*: grief, distress.

One of the four chief passions: the false judgment that what is being experienced now is an evil. The Stoics described it as a contraction of the soul, the opposite of the expansion in pleasure. In [[02-10|2.10]] Marcus, following Theophrastus, notes that the angry man turns from reason "with a certain pain and unconscious contraction," that is, he describes anger in the language of the school. Main places: [[02-10|2.10]], [[04-03|4.3]], [[04-49|4.49]], [[06-18|6.18]], [[09-40|9.40]], [[10-25|10.25]], [[10-28|10.28]], [[11-18|11.18]] and elsewhere.

### μεταβολή

*metabolē*: change, transformation.

For the Stoics the world exists by a cycle of transformations: fire passes into air, air into water and back again, and the birth and death of a single thing are only links in this cycle. Marcus returns to this thought constantly in order to remove the fear of death: the universe is preserved by changes ([[02-03|2.3]]), and nature "loves nothing so much as to change the things which are and to make new things like them" ([[04-36|4.36]]). Main places: [[02-17|2.17]], [[04-03|4.3]], [[04-14|4.14]], [[04-36|4.36]], [[05-23|5.23]], [[06-04|6.4]], [[07-47|7.47]], [[09-01|9.1]] and elsewhere.

### μοῖρα

*moira*: portion, lot.

What is measured out. For the Stoics the same as fate, but taken as the separate part that falls to each. Marcus advises accepting one's portion as part of the common order ([[03-04|3.4]]) and willingly giving oneself up to Clotho, one of the three Fates ([[04-34|4.34]]). Main places: [[02-05|2.5]], [[02-06|2.6]], [[03-04|3.4]], [[03-16|3.16]], [[04-34|4.34]], [[05-37|5.37]].

### νοῦς

*nous*: mind, intelligence.

Man's highest faculty. For the Stoics each man's mind is a fragment of the divine reason of the world and therefore common to all men: on this their kinship rests. Marcus often says τὸ νοερόν, "the intelligent"; in [[05-27|5.27]] every man's understanding and reason is called the daemon that Zeus gave him "for his guardian and guide." Main places: [[02-01|2.1]], [[03-03|3.3]], [[04-05|4.5]], [[05-27|5.27]], [[10-11|10.11]], [[10-33|10.33]], [[11-01|11.1]], [[12-03|12.3]] and elsewhere.

### ὁρμή

*hormē*: impulse.

In the Stoic sequence an impression is followed by assent, assent by impulse and action; impulse follows from a judgment already accepted, and so one is answerable for it. For Marcus what matters is where an impulse is directed: toward the common good or against it. The ruling faculty must not be "pulled by the strings like a puppet to unsocial movements" ([[02-02|2.2]]). Long renders ὁρμή variously as "movement," "impulse" and "desire." Main places: [[02-02|2.2]], [[03-01|3.1]], [[03-06|3.6]], [[06-16|6.16]], [[07-04|7.4]], [[08-01|8.1]], [[08-07|8.7]], [[09-01|9.1]] and elsewhere.

### πάθος

*pathos*: passion.

Not every feeling, but an irrational movement of the soul born of assent to a false judgment about good or evil. The four chief passions are desire, fear, pleasure and grief. The ideal is not insensibility but freedom from such assents (see ἀπάθεια). In [[02-05|2.5]] Marcus speaks of "passionate aversion from the commands of reason." Main places: [[02-05|2.5]], [[02-13|2.13]], [[03-04|3.4]], [[08-48|8.48]], [[12-19|12.19]].

### τὸ παρόν

*to paron*: the present.

The past is gone, the future is not yet; only the present moment belongs to a man. Hence the argument of [[02-14|2.14]]: a long life and a short one lose the same thing, "for what a man has not, how can any one take this from him?" Marcus returns himself again and again to the present and to what must be done in it. Main places: [[02-14|2.14]], [[03-10|3.10]], [[03-12|3.12]], [[06-02|6.2]], [[06-32|6.32]], [[12-01|12.1]], [[12-03|12.3]], [[12-26|12.26]] and elsewhere.

### πνεῦμα

*pneuma*: breath, spirit.

For the Stoics a fine mixture of fire and air that pervades and holds together the whole world, and in man makes up the soul. In the narrow sense simply breath. Marcus deliberately reduces pneuma to the narrow sense: in [[02-02|2.2]] he uses the diminutive πνευμάτιον, "a little breath," and calls it "air, and not always the same, but every moment sent out and again sucked in," so as not to take it for himself. Main places: [[02-02|2.2]], [[04-03|4.3]], [[04-04|4.4]], [[09-36|9.36]], [[11-20|11.20]], [[12-03|12.3]], [[12-14|12.14]], [[12-30|12.30]].

### προαίρεσις

*prohairesis*: choice, the faculty of choice.

Epictetus's key word: the only thing truly "ours," which no external force can compel. Marcus uses it rarely, preferring to speak of the ruling faculty, the daemon and the mind. In [[06-41|6.41]] he warns that whoever counts as good or evil "the things which are not within thy power" will inevitably "blame the gods, and hate men too." In [[08-56|8.56]] Long renders the related προαιρετικόν "free will." Main places: [[03-06|3.6]], [[06-41|6.41]], [[08-56|8.56]], [[11-36|11.36]], [[12-03|12.3]], [[12-23|12.23]], [[12-33|12.33]].

### πρόνοια

*pronoia*: providence.

The world is not arranged by blind chance but thought out beforehand for the sake of the whole. For the Stoics it is the same as the nature of the whole and fate, but with a shade of care. Marcus often sets two possibilities side by side: "either there is providence or atoms" ([[04-03|4.3]], [[06-10|6.10]]). Main places: [[02-03|2.3]], [[02-11|2.11]], [[04-03|4.3]], [[06-10|6.10]], [[06-44|6.44]], [[12-01|12.1]], [[12-14|12.14]], [[12-24|12.24]].

### σάρξ

*sarx*: flesh.

Marcus usually speaks of it in the diminutive, σαρκίδιον, and in [[02-02|2.2]] σαρκία, "a little flesh": the body is not evil, but neither is it "I"; it is material. In [[02-02|2.2]] man is taken apart into flesh, breath and the ruling part, and the flesh is described as "blood and bones and network, a contexture of nerves, veins, and arteries." Main places: [[02-02|2.2]], [[06-28|6.28]], [[10-08|10.8]], [[10-24|10.24]], [[12-01|12.1]], [[12-14|12.14]].

### σκοπός

*skopos*: aim, target.

Literally an archer's target. The Stoics distinguished σκοπός and τέλος as the target and hitting it. Marcus speaks both of the aim of a single act ([[07-04|7.4]]) and of the one aim of a whole life ([[11-21|11.21]]). Without an aim, Marcus says, a man rushes about and spins in vain ([[02-07|2.7]]), and the aim should be social and civic ([[11-21|11.21]]). Main places: [[02-07|2.7]], [[02-16|2.16]], [[07-04|7.4]], [[11-06|11.6]], [[11-21|11.21]].

### στοιχεῖα

*stoicheia*: elements.

The four elements: earth, water, air and fire; for the Stoics the world lives by their mutual transformations (see μεταβολή). Marcus reminds himself that death is "a dissolution of the elements of which every living being is compounded," and since the elements themselves take no harm from it, there is nothing to fear ([[02-17|2.17]]). Main places: [[02-03|2.3]], [[02-17|2.17]], [[04-04|4.4]], [[04-05|4.5]], [[04-32|4.32]], [[04-46|4.46]], [[07-47|7.47]], [[10-07|10.7]] and elsewhere.

### συγγενής

*syngenēs*: kin, akin.

Men are akin not by blood and seed but by sharing in mind and in the divine portion ([[02-01|2.1]]); so one cannot be angry with a kinsman or hate him. Marcus reminds himself of this kinship precisely when another does wrong ([[07-22|7.22]]). Main places: [[02-01|2.1]], [[02-13|2.13]], [[03-04|3.4]], [[03-11|3.11]], [[07-22|7.22]], [[09-09|9.9]], [[09-22|9.22]], [[12-26|12.26]] and elsewhere.

### συγκατάθεσις

*synkatathesis*: assent.

The act by which the ruling faculty accepts an impression as true. Impressions come of themselves, but assent is in our power; so the Stoics saw in it the chief moral act. In [[11-37|11.37]] Marcus copies the words of Epictetus that "a man must discover an art with respect to giving his assent." Main places: [[04-03|4.3]], [[05-10|5.10]], [[11-37|11.37]].

### συνεργία

*synergia*: co-operation.

Men are made for co-operation, "like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the rows of the upper and lower teeth" ([[02-01|2.1]]); acting against one another is contrary to nature (see ἀντιπράσσειν). Marcus adds that we all work toward one end, and even sleepers, as Heraclitus said, are "labourers and co-operators in the things which take place in the universe" ([[06-42|6.42]]). Main places: [[02-01|2.1]], [[06-14|6.14]], [[06-42|6.42]], [[06-43|6.43]], [[07-13|7.13]], [[07-19|7.19]], [[09-40|9.40]], [[09-42|9.42]].

### σωφροσύνη

*sōphrosynē*: temperance, moderation.

One of the four cardinal virtues: the knowledge of what to choose and what to avoid. It keeps impulses within measure not by an effort of will but by a right valuation, under which a superfluous desire does not arise. In [[05-12|5.12]] temperance stands among the true goods, to which the comic poet's jibe, fitting for wealth and luxury, does not stick. Main places: [[05-12|5.12]], [[07-63|7.63]], [[12-15|12.15]], [[12-27|12.27]].

### τέλος

*telos*: end, final goal.

That for the sake of which everything else is done. The Stoics defined the final goal as life according to nature. In [[05-16|5.16]] Marcus reasons that each being is carried toward that for which it was made, "and where the end is, there also is the advantage and the good of each thing"; for a rational being this is society. Main places: [[03-14|3.14]], [[05-14|5.14]], [[05-15|5.15]], [[05-16|5.16]], [[09-23|9.23]], [[11-01|11.1]], [[12-20|12.20]].

### τιμή

*timē*: honor.

External honor, the recognition of others, belongs for the Stoics to the indifferent. What matters is the honor the rational soul pays itself. In [[02-06|2.6]] Marcus reproaches his soul that soon it "wilt no longer have the opportunity of honouring thyself," and in [[06-16|6.16]] he asks what is worth valuing and answers that it is not the applause of the many. Main places: [[02-06|2.6]], [[06-16|6.16]].

### τύχη

*tychē*: chance, fortune.

For the Stoics there are no true accidents: we call chance a connection of causes we do not see. In [[02-03|2.3]] Marcus writes that "that which is from fortune is not separated from nature or without an interweaving and involution with the things which are ordered by Providence." Main places: [[02-03|2.3]], [[02-17|2.17]], [[03-11|3.11]], [[12-27|12.27]].

### ὑπεξαίρεσις

*hypexairesis*: reservation.

To strive for an external aim in earnest, but with the condition "if nothing prevents." The outcome is left in advance among the indifferent, so failure does not shake inner calm. Marcus connects the reservation with the image of fire, which makes fuel of whatever is thrown into it ([[04-01|4.1]]): a hindrance becomes material for action ([[05-20|5.20]]). In [[11-37|11.37]] Long renders it "with regard to circumstances." Main places: [[04-01|4.1]], [[05-20|5.20]], [[06-50|6.50]], [[11-37|11.37]].

### ὑπόληψις

*hypolēpsis*: judgment, opinion.

The valuation that the ruling faculty adds to an impression. What makes us unhappy is the judgment, not the thing, and the judgment is in our power. Hence Marcus's thought "that all is opinion" ([[02-15|2.15]]) and his short rule: "Take away thy opinion, and then there is taken away the complaint, 'I have been harmed.' Take away the complaint, 'I have been harmed,' and the harm is taken away" ([[04-07|4.7]]). Main places: [[04-03|4.3]], [[04-07|4.7]], [[07-02|7.2]], [[07-16|7.16]], [[09-06|9.6]], [[09-32|9.32]], [[10-11|10.11]], [[10-33|10.33]] and elsewhere.

### φαντασία

*phantasia*: impression, appearance.

What appears to consciousness before any judgment about it. An impression in itself is neither good nor bad: everything depends on the assent the ruling faculty gives it. Marcus's exercises are built on work with impressions: to add nothing of one's own to what the first appearances report ([[08-49|8.49]]), and to wipe away what is troublesome ([[05-02|5.2]]). Main places: [[02-05|2.5]], [[03-01|3.1]], [[03-04|3.4]], [[03-06|3.6]], [[05-16|5.16]], [[06-13|6.13]], [[06-16|6.16]], [[06-36|6.36]] and elsewhere.

### φιλοστοργία

*philostorgia*: natural affection.

Warm love, as between parents and children. The Stoics extended it to all men as to kin, because all rational beings are parts of one whole. It is not in the classical list of virtues, but Marcus sets it in a row with dignity, freedom and justice ([[02-05|2.5]]) and recalls that Sextus was "most affectionate" ([[01-09|1.9]]). Main places: [[01-09|1.9]], [[01-17|1.17]], [[02-05|2.5]], [[11-18|11.18]].

### φρόνησις

*phronēsis*: wisdom, practical wisdom.

The first of the four cardinal virtues: the knowledge of what should and should not be done. The other virtues can be understood as the same wisdom applied to their own domains. In [[05-09|5.9]] Marcus asks what could be more agreeable than wisdom itself. In [[05-12|5.12]] Long renders φρόνησις "prudence." Main places: [[05-09|5.9]], [[05-12|5.12]], [[05-18|5.18]].

### φύσις

*physis*: nature.

The word is used in three senses: the nature of the whole (the same as god and the reason of the world), the nature of man (rationality and sociability), and the nature of a single thing. The Stoic rule "to live according to nature" joins all three. It seems to be the most frequent of these words in Marcus. What is contrary to nature breaks the bond with people ([[02-01|2.1]]), and death is a work of nature ([[02-12|2.12]]). Main places: [[01-17|1.17]], [[02-01|2.1]], [[02-17|2.17]], [[03-02|3.2]], [[03-04|3.4]], [[04-05|4.5]], [[04-36|4.36]], [[04-48|4.48]] and elsewhere.

### χαρά

*chara*: rational joy.

The Stoics opposed it to pleasure as a passion (see ἡδονή): the sage rejoices in the true good, that is, in virtue, his own and others', and such joy does not depend on circumstances. Marcus speaks of joy with the verbs εὐφραίνειν and χαίρειν: one is delighted by the virtues of those with whom one lives ([[06-48|6.48]]) and by a sound ruling faculty ([[08-43|8.43]]). Main places: [[06-48|6.48]], [[08-26|8.26]], [[08-43|8.43]].

### ψυχή

*psychē*: soul.

The soul as a whole: for the Stoics a warm pneuma that animates the body; the ruling faculty is only its highest part. Marcus often addresses his own soul in the second person to admonish it ([[02-06|2.6]]), and after Epictetus calls man "a little soul bearing about a corpse" ([[04-41|4.41]]). Main places: [[04-03|4.3]], [[05-16|5.16]], [[06-32|6.32]], [[07-16|7.16]], [[08-28|8.28]], [[11-01|11.1]], [[11-16|11.16]], [[12-07|12.7]] and elsewhere.
