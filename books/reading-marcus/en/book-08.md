## Line of the Book

**A Philosopher Despite His Station.** Book VIII opens with an admission: Marcus can no longer live the life of a philosopher, and his own station stands in the way (8.1). What remains is what no station can take away: sound judgment, the common work, and a mind that cannot be taken by storm.

Twelve key passages: too late for a philosopher's fame (8.1) → conquerors and philosophers (8.3) → first calm, then the work (8.5) → the three conditions of the right way (8.7) → five questions to put to a thing (8.11) → a second morning (8.12) → turn the body inside out (8.21) → pain and the soul (8.28) → at the tomb of Verus (8.37) → three sources of grief (8.47) → add nothing (8.49) → another's will (8.56).

## Introduction

Book VIII is full of admissions one does not expect from an emperor. "Thou hast not leisure or ability to read," he tells himself in 8.8, and at once lists what he still has leisure for. In 8.9 he forbids himself to complain about life at court: "Let no man any longer hear thee finding fault with the court life or with thy own." In 8.25, 8.31 and 8.37 he recalls the dead of his own circle and of the court of Augustus.

This book also gave Pierre Hadot the title of his study of Marcus, *The Inner Citadel*. In 8.48 we read that "the mind which is free from passions is a citadel, for man has nothing more secure to which he can fly for refuge." The Greek word is ἀκρόπολις, the fortress on the height of a city. Marcus's image of the inner life keeps changing: in [[04-03|4.3]] it is a retreat, in [[07-59|7.59]] a spring, and here a citadel.

The passages fall into several themes.

- **A philosopher at court:** 8.1, 8.8, 8.9, 8.30, 8.44, 8.53.
- **Judgment and impressions:** 8.7, 8.11, 8.13, 8.14, 8.29, 8.40, 8.47, 8.49.
- **The mind as a citadel:** 8.28, 8.41, 8.45, 8.48, 8.56, 8.57, 8.60.
- **Death and oblivion:** 8.3, 8.5, 8.21, 8.25, 8.31, 8.37, 8.58.
- **The whole and nature:** 8.6, 8.18, 8.19, 8.20, 8.34, 8.46, 8.50, 8.52, 8.54, 8.55.
- **The work and other people:** 8.2, 8.12, 8.15, 8.17, 8.23, 8.26, 8.32, 8.59, 8.61.

Among the passages without commentary, two deserve notice. In 8.50: "A cucumber is bitter.—Throw it away.—There are briars in the road.—Turn aside from them." And in 8.59 the whole ethics of living with others fits into two sentences: "Men exist for the sake of one another. Teach them then or bear with them."

## 8.1

![[08-01#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

The emperor admits that he can no longer win the reputation of a philosopher: "thy plan of life also opposes it." The Greek is ἡ ὑπόθεσις, his position: rank, duties, the court. With so much power in his hands, he might, it seems, have left his duties to others, withdrawn from affairs and given himself to a quiet life of contemplation. The Stoics taught otherwise. In the words of Zeno that Seneca reports (*On Leisure* 3.2), the wise man "will take part in public affairs unless something prevents him" (my translation), and a philosopher does not shirk the duties laid upon him.

In antiquity a philosopher was not someone who wrote treatises but someone who had chosen the philosophical way of life. Marcus admits that it is plain to him and to others that he has not lived that way. He is probably too hard on himself: his contemporaries already saw in him a philosopher on the throne.

Since the reputation of a philosopher is out of reach, he can stop caring about it: "throw away the thought, How thou shalt seem to others, and be content if thou shalt live the rest of thy life in such wise as thy nature wills." Long follows the Greek closely here: τὸ μὲν τί δόξεις ἄφες, "let go of how you will seem." Epictetus says the same: "Be satisfied then in everything with being a philosopher; and if you wish to seem also to any person to be a philosopher, appear so to yourself, and you will be able to do this" (*Enchiridion* 23, trans. Long).

Where, then, is the good life to be found? Marcus runs through the places he has looked: "not in syllogisms, nor in wealth, nor in reputation, nor in enjoyment, nor anywhere." What remains is "doing what man's nature requires." For that one needs principles "from which come his affects and his acts," and the chief of them is "the belief that there is nothing good for man, which does not make him just, temperate, manly, free."

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^virtue-is-sufficient|Virtue is sufficient for happiness]]: nothing is good except what makes a man just, temperate, brave and free. The good life that Marcus did not find in syllogisms or in fame consists in [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|living according to nature]].

## 8.3

![[08-03#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

Alexander, Caesar and Pompey conquered nations; Diogenes lived in poverty, and Socrates was put to death by his fellow citizens. Marcus sets them side by side, and the question already suggests its answer: the fame of rulers fades beside those who were masters not of lands but of themselves.

Why are the philosophers greater? "They were acquainted with things, and their causes (forms), and their matter." This is the stripped view of [[06-13|6.13]] and [[07-29|7.29]]: whoever sees a thing through to its material and its cause cannot be enslaved by it. That is why "the ruling principles of these men were the same," that is, they stayed true to themselves.

"But as to the others, how many things had they to care for, and to how many things were they slaves!" Long keeps the Greek image exactly: δουλεία, slavery. The conqueror of the world turns out to be the slave of countless cares. Alexander, Caesar and Pompey have already appeared in [[03-03|3.3]], among those who themselves in the end departed from life.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^self-is-hegemonikon|The true self is the ruling faculty]], and freedom lies in its staying true to itself, not in power over others.

## 8.5

![[08-05#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

First calm, then the work: that is the order in which Marcus instructs himself. "This is the chief thing: Be not perturbed, for all things are according to the nature of the universal; and in a little time thou wilt be nobody and nowhere, like Hadrian and Augustus." Two arguments come at once: what happens is lawful, and the one who frets will not last long.

The names are not chosen at random. Augustus was the first emperor; Hadrian adopted Antoninus and so was Marcus's grandfather by adoption. If even they are "nobody and nowhere," the reputation of a philosopher, discussed in [[08-01|8.1]], is all the less worth worrying about.

Then comes the work: "having fixed thy eyes steadily on thy business look at it." Remember "that it is thy duty to be a good man," and do what man's nature demands "without turning aside." The Greek ἀμεταστρεπτί means "without looking back," as one walks a road without turning around. At the end Marcus says how this is to be done: "only let it be with a good disposition and with modesty and without hypocrisy."

**Doctrines.** Man's work is to [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|live according to nature]] "without turning aside." And calm rests on [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|acceptance of fate]]: all that happens accords with the nature of the whole, and there is no reason to be perturbed by it.

## 8.6

![[08-06#Английский]]

## 8.7

![[08-07#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

Pierre Hadot built his reading of the *Meditations* on this passage, and for understanding Marcus's spiritual practice it is perhaps the most important of all. "Every nature is contented with itself when it goes on its way well." What does it mean for a rational nature to go on its way well? Marcus names three conditions.

First, that "in its thoughts it assents to nothing false or uncertain": this is the discipline of assent. Second, that "it directs its movements to social acts only": the discipline of action. Third, that "it confines its desires and aversions to the things which are in its power, and... is satisfied with everything that is assigned to it by the common nature": the discipline of desire. Assent, action and desire are the three disciplines around which, according to Hadot, the *Meditations* are built.

Then comes a comparison: each particular nature is part of the common nature, "as the nature of the leaf is a part of the nature of the plant." The leaf belongs to a nature "which has not perception or reason, and is subject to be impeded"; man belongs to a nature "which is not subject to impediments, and is intelligent and just." Anything can hinder a leaf; nothing hinders the nature of which man is a part, and so his reason too can be free of hindrance.

**Doctrines.** Desire and aversion are directed only to what depends on us, and for that one must [[app-d-map-of-practice#^dichotomy-of-control|distinguish what is in our power from what is not]]. The right way of a rational being is [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|life according to nature]]. And what the common nature sends is accepted without complaint: this is [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|acceptance of fate]].

## 8.11

![[08-11#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

The passage consists entirely of questions, and they can be put to anything: an office, an insult, a purchase, one's own fear.

This is the purest formula of the stripped view of [[06-13|6.13]]. There Marcus applied it to wine and purple; here he gives the method itself: what the thing is in itself, what it is made of, what brought it about, what it does in the world, and how long it lasts. A thing taken apart by these questions loses its hold on the soul.

The last question is: "And how long does it subsist?" The stripped view includes time as well: every thing, however precious, has its term.

**Doctrines.** It is judgment that sets the value of a thing, and judgment is [[app-d-map-of-practice#^dichotomy-of-control|in our power]]; the stripped view returns the thing to what it is.

## 8.12

![[08-12#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

"When thou risest from sleep with reluctance, remember": so begins the second morning meditation in the *Meditations*, after [[05-01|5.1]]. Against the reluctance to rise Marcus sets two arguments: man's purpose is to "perform social acts," whereas sleeping "is common also to irrational animals." The choice between sleep and work is thus presented as a choice between the animal and the human in oneself.

Marcus then answers the objection of laziness from [[05-01|5.1]]: "But this is more pleasant." What accords with each nature "is also more peculiarly its own, and more suitable to its nature, and indeed also more agreeable." Long's "more peculiarly its own" renders the Greek οἰκειότερον exactly. From the same root comes the Stoic οἰκείωσις, "appropriation," the doctrine that every living thing from birth reaches for what is its own. For man what is his own is the common good, while sleep he shares with the animals. Hence the answer to "more pleasant": for the Stoics pleasure is not the goal a living thing strives for but what follows when a nature obtains what is its own. Diogenes Laertius reports their teaching thus: "pleasure, if it is really felt, they declare to be a by-product" (VII.86, trans. Hicks); the Greek is ἐπιγέννημα. Whoever does his own work finds it pleasant.

A thought about measure belongs here too. Sleep is necessary, but even so natural a need has a measure, and in [[05-01|5.1]] Marcus says plainly that "nature has fixed bounds to this too: she has fixed bounds both to eating and drinking." Sleep must be proportioned to activity, and in this temperance shows itself.

On the reluctance to rise Seneca speaks more simply: "we are better men if we anticipate the day and welcome the dawn," and "we are base churls if we lie dozing when the sun is high in the heavens" (Letters 122.1, trans. Gummere).

**Doctrines.** For man, to [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|live according to nature]] means to labor for the common work. And [[app-d-map-of-practice#^oikeiosis|men are born for one another]], so the common good is what is most truly a man's own.

**Commentary.**

Marcus applies two devices against self-deception. The first concerns the body: "Turn it (the body) inside out, and see what kind of thing it is; and when it has grown old, what kind of thing it becomes, and when it is diseased." The stripped view takes away the body's charm.

The second concerns fame. "Short-lived are both the praiser and the praised, and the rememberer and the remembered": all four roles are brief. And all this happens "in a nook of this part of the world," where people agree neither with one another nor each with himself. "And the whole earth too is a point." We have met this thought already in [[04-03|4.3]]. Fame, then, is small in time and small in space.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^fame-is-empty|Fame is empty]]: short-lived are both those who praise and those who are praised.

**Commentary.**

Two cases are considered. If pain is an evil to the body, "then let the body say what it thinks of it." If to the soul, the soul can refuse to agree: it has the power "to maintain its own serenity and tranquility." The two Greek words are αἰθρία and γαλήνη, a cloudless sky and a calm sea.

Why is this possible? Because "every judgement and movement and desire and aversion is within, and no evil ascends so high." Pain knocks from outside, but it does not of itself enter the place where we judge and decide; only our assent can let it in. This continues [[07-16|7.16]] and [[07-64|7.64]].

**Doctrines.** Pain is not in our power, but [[app-d-map-of-practice#^dichotomy-of-control|our judgment about pain is]]. [[app-d-map-of-practice#^self-is-hegemonikon|The true self is the reason]], and pain does not reach it.

**Commentary.**

"Does Panthea or Pergamus now sit by the tomb of Verus? Does Chabrias or Diotimus sit by the tomb of Hadrian?" The Verus here is Lucius Verus, Marcus's adoptive brother and co-emperor, who died in 169; Panthea was his mistress, whose beauty Lucian praised. Marcus, of course, knew Verus's circle well.

Then comes a ladder of questions. "That would be ridiculous." But suppose they did sit there: "would the dead be conscious of it? And if the dead were conscious, would they be pleased? And if they were pleased, would that make them immortal?" No: "Was it not in the order of destiny that these persons too should first become old women and old men and then die?" Posthumous devotion does not reach the one it honors and changes nothing in his fate or in the fate of the mourners.

"All this is foul smell and blood in a bag." For "blood" the Greek has λύθρος, the same word as in [[03-03|3.3]]; in Homer it is the gore spattered on warriors in battle, so Long's "blood" softens it a little. This is the stripped view applied to the body of the honored and of those who honor him alike. And its harshness is turned on Marcus's own people: he himself buried his adoptive brother.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^fame-is-empty|Fame is empty]], even the memory kept by those closest to us. And death, appointed for all, is accepted without clinging to the memory of oneself: this is [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|acceptance of fate]].

## 8.47

**Commentary.**

Marcus goes through the sources of grief. If something external pains you, "it is not this thing that disturbs thee, but thy own judgement about it," and "it is in thy power to wipe out this judgement now." The Greek ἐξαλεῖψαι, "wipe out," is the same verb as in [[07-29|7.29]]. If something in your own disposition pains you, "who hinders thee from correcting thy opinion?" And if you are pained because you are not doing what seems right, "why dost thou not rather act than complain?"

"But some insuperable obstacle is in the way?" Then do not grieve, "for the cause of its not being done depends not on thee": you have done what you could. "But it is not worth while to live if this cannot be done." Then, Marcus answers, take your departure from life, but "contentedly... and well pleased too with the things which are obstacles." Where Long has "contentedly" and "well pleased," the Greek twice uses εὐμενής, "well disposed, kindly." This is the Stoic "open door" of [[05-29|5.29]], and here again the point is not the departure itself but the absence of resentment, toward the obstacle and toward life.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^dichotomy-of-control|Our judgment and our action are in our power, the obstacle is not]], and there is no need to grieve over it.

## 8.48

![[08-48#Английский]]

## 8.49

![[08-49#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

The first words are "Say nothing more to thyself." The passage is not about what happens around us but about what a person says inside. Between an impression and a feeling there is almost always a short sentence the mind adds on its own: "this is bad," "I have been wronged," "all is lost." We hardly notice it, yet it is what makes the event an evil; remove it, and the distress goes with it.

Marcus gives two examples. The first is built like a messenger's report: ἤγγελται τοῦτο, "this has been reported"; ὅτι βέβλαψαι, οὐκ ἤγγελται, "that thou hast been injured, that has not been reported." The injury, then, you added yourself. In the second, the child is sick, that much is visible, "but that he is in danger, I do not see": danger is already a forecast built by fear. Here Marcus writes as a father who lost many of his children in infancy. He does not forbid himself to care for the child; he separates what he sees from what fear paints in.

Hence the conclusion: "always abide by the first appearances, and add nothing thyself from within." Long's "add" renders ἐπιλέγειν, literally "to say in addition." It is the same rule as in the words of Epicurus quoted in [[07-64|7.64]], and the same mechanism as in [[04-07|4.7]]: take away the judgment, and the harm is taken away. Marcus probably took the exercise from Epictetus, whose *Discourses* (III.8) contain almost the same lesson: a son has died, a ship has been lost, a man has been led to prison, "But that herein he has fared badly, every man adds from his own opinion" (trans. Long).

There is one qualification: "Or rather add something, like a man who knows everything that happens in the world." One may add, then, if the addition comes from knowledge of nature and not from fear: a child's illness is an event in the order of the world, not a catastrophe.

**Doctrines.** The impression comes of itself, but [[app-d-map-of-practice#^dichotomy-of-control|what we add to it is in our power]].

## 8.56

**Commentary.**

"To my own free will the free will of my neighbour is just as indifferent as his poor breath and flesh." Long's "free will" stands for the Greek προαιρετικόν, the faculty of choice, ordinarily the most valuable thing in a person. Yet another's will is in his power, not in mine, and so for my good it is as indifferent as his body.

Marcus grants that "we are made especially for the sake of one another," but "the ruling power of each of us has its own office," and another's vice does not enter it.

Otherwise, he says, "my neighbour's wickedness would be my harm, which God has not willed in order that my unhappiness may not depend on another." This is not indifference to people, of whom 8.59 says: "Teach them then or bear with them." It is a refusal to hand another the key to one's own happiness.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^no-harm-to-virtuous|No harm can be done to the virtuous]]: another's vice is not my evil. Another's will is [[app-d-map-of-practice#^dichotomy-of-control|not in my power]]. And [[app-d-map-of-practice#^providence|the world is ordered by providence]] so that each person's happiness depends on himself.

## The Book in Brief

**Key thought.** His station took from Marcus the life of a philosopher, but not his judgment. The key passages of Book VIII converge on one point: between an event and a feeling stands what the mind adds on its own, and that addition is in our power. What pains us is not the external thing but our judgment about it ([[08-47|8.47]]). It was reported that someone speaks ill of you; it was not reported that you have been injured ([[08-49|8.49]]). Pain does not enter the place where we judge and decide ([[08-28|8.28]]).

**Practice: attending to the movements of the soul (παρακολούθησις).** Marcus gives the formula himself: "always abide by the first appearances, and add nothing thyself from within." The exercise is to notice the moment when a judgment is added to what was reported, and to name for oneself what was given and what was added. It is easiest to begin with small things, while little is at stake: a remark from someone else, a delay, a piece of bad news. Usually it turns out that what was added is longer than what was given, and that everything unpleasant lies in the addition.
