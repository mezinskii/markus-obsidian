## Line of the Book

**Only the Present.** In seventeen passages Book II runs through almost all the exercises to which Marcus Aurelius will return later.

Five key passages: how to meet the morning (2.1) → what I am made of and what I really command (2.2) → how to do what is in hand (2.5) → why one can live only in the present (2.14) → what remains when everything flows (2.17). The book ends with a note, "This in Carnuntum," that is, in the military camp on the Danube: this book too, it seems, like the first, was written at war. Some editors, however, take the note as the heading of Book III.

## Introduction

The exercises proper begin with Book II. After the thanks of Book I we hear for the first time the voice that will sound to the end of the text: Marcus addresses himself as "thou," gives orders, checks himself, repeats: "Throw away thy books," "Thou art an old man," "Remember how long thou hast been putting off these things."

The themes of the book are these. Preparing in the morning to meet unpleasant people (2.1). Dividing oneself into flesh, breath and the ruling faculty (2.2). Providence and the order of the whole (2.3). Enough putting off (2.4). Every act as the last (2.5). Do not neglect yourself (2.6). Do not wander (2.7). Do not peer into other souls; watch your own (2.8). Remember the nature of the whole (2.9). Faults from desire are worse than faults from anger (2.10). Live as one who may depart at any moment (2.11). How quickly everything vanishes (2.12). Guard the daemon within (2.13). One can lose only the present (2.14). Everything is opinion (2.15). How the soul disgraces itself (2.16). What can conduct a man through life (2.17).

Almost every one of these themes will return dozens of times, in other words and from another side. From this book on, the commentaries note which doctrines appear in a passage; the full list is in the [[app-d-map-of-practice|map of practice]]. Among the passages without commentary, note 2.4, in which Marcus reproaches himself for putting things off; he will return to this thought in [[03-14|3.14]] and [[04-17|4.17]].

In 2.2 Marcus tells himself: "Thou art an old man." He is probably about fifty, and he is writing, to judge by the note at the end of the book, in a military camp on the Danube.

## 2.1

![[02-01#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

Before a hard day begins, Marcus prepares himself for it. This is the morning meditation, one of the best-known passages of the *Meditations*.

The first words in Greek are Ἕωθεν προλέγειν ἑαυτῷ, "at dawn to say to oneself beforehand," and Long renders them well: "Begin the morning by saying to thyself." Marcus names to himself the encounters that await him. The Roman Stoics called this exercise the anticipation of evils (*praemeditatio malorum*): a trouble seen in advance no longer takes one by surprise.

Then comes the list of unpleasant people: "the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial." Most likely these are not abstract vices but people the emperor meets every day: petitioners, courtiers, senators. "Envious" is the Greek βάσκανος, a word of the same root as βασκανία. In [[01-11|1.11]] Marcus thanks Fronto for teaching him "to observe what envy, and duplicity, and hypocrisy are in a tyrant," and the "envy" there is precisely βασκανία; Long renders the root the same way in both places.

The exercise is to change one's view of these people before meeting them, and it goes through three steps. First: all this happens to them "by reason of their ignorance of what is good and evil." This is the thought of Socrates that no one does wrong willingly, only by mistake; one does not grow angry with a man who errs, but pities or corrects him. Second: he is **akin** to me, "not only of the same blood or seed, but that it participates in the same intelligence and the same portion of the divinity." Long follows the Greek closely here, νοῦ καὶ θείας ἀπομοίρας: all men share in one reason. Third: they cannot harm me, "for no one can fix on me what is ugly." No one can draw me into what is shameful unless I consent, and what does not disgrace me is not harm.

For one image alone this passage is worth learning by heart: "we are made for co-operation, like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the rows of the upper and lower teeth." The rows of teeth work against each other, and it is just this opposition that lets them chew. To be angry with a man and turn away from him is like one hand making war on the other, and that is contrary to nature.

The passage ends with the words: "it is acting against one another to be vexed and to turn away." So even silent irritation, it seems, is already a kind of enmity. Marcus asks more of himself than to put up with unpleasant people: not to separate himself from them.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^no-harm-to-virtuous|No harm can be done to the virtuous]]: the one who errs harms himself, not me. [[app-d-map-of-practice#^unity-of-cosmos|All things are bound together by one reason]]. [[app-d-map-of-practice#^oikeiosis|Men are born for one another]]; the Stoics called this οἰκείωσις, "appropriation," which widens from oneself to the whole community of rational beings.

## 2.2

![[02-02#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

Marcus asks himself what he is made of and what of it he really commands.

There are three parts, and in the Greek they are named with deliberate disdain: σαρκία, a diminutive of "flesh," something like "a bit of meat," and πνευμάτιον, "a little breath." Long's "a little flesh and breath" keeps the tone. The diminutives are there on purpose: Marcus strips the body and life of their importance, speaking of them as something small and passing. The third part is τὸ ἡγεμονικόν, "the ruling part," the reason that judges, decides and assents, and this he does not call by a diminutive.

A sudden shout follows: "Throw away thy books; no longer distract thyself: it is not allowed." In Greek it is sharper still: ἄφες τὰ βιβλία, "let the books go"; μηκέτι σπῶ, "stop letting yourself be dragged about." Marcus, it seems, loved books: in the next passage ([[02-03|2.3]]) he tells himself to "cast away the thirst after books." And still here he tells himself that it is too late to read and time to act. He writes the same in [[03-14|3.14]]: his notes, perhaps these very ones, he will not have time to read.

The body, Marcus says, "is blood and bones and network, a contexture of nerves, veins, and arteries"; the breath is "air, and not always the same, but every moment sent out and again sucked in." What remains truly his own, then, is only the ruling faculty.

He then explains what it means to care for the ruling faculty: "no longer let this be a slave, no longer be pulled by the strings like a puppet to unsocial movements." Long keeps the image exactly: the Greek νευροσπαστηθῆναι is how a puppet moves when its strings are pulled; the impulse jerks, and the man jerks. Finally: no longer "be either dissatisfied with thy present lot, or shrink from the future." This last point is the chief one: both complaint and fear mean that the mind has left the present; the book will return to this thought in [[02-14|2.14]].

The words "Thou art an old man" serve here as an argument: at these years it is shameful to let oneself be a puppet.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^self-is-hegemonikon|A man's true self is the ruling faculty]], not the body and not the breath. [[app-d-map-of-practice#^dichotomy-of-control|Only what this reason does is in our power]]; everything else does not depend on us. And there is no reason to complain of the present: what nature assigns is accepted, and in this lies [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|acceptance of fate]].

## 2.5

![[02-05#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

If 2.1 teaches how to prepare for a new day, 2.5 teaches how to live each of its hours. The first words of the passage are Πάσης ὥρας, "every hour"; Long has "Every moment."

The key phrase is "what thou hast in hand," and Long renders the Greek τὸ ἐν χερσί exactly: what is in one's hands, that is, attention to the present. It is to be done "as a Roman and a man," "with perfect and simple dignity, and feeling of affection, and freedom, and justice."

Marcus tells himself to "give thyself relief from all other thoughts." The Greek is σχολὴν σαυτῷ πορίζειν, "to provide oneself with leisure" from everything else. Usually leisure is sought outside the work, when it is finished; Marcus finds it in the work itself: full attention to one task is rest from all the others.

This is achieved by doing every act "as if it were the last." It is a way of gathering one's attention: a last act is not done carelessly, with distraction, thinking of the next. Marcus adds a list of what such an act is free from: "carelessness and passionate aversion from the commands of reason, and all hypocrisy, and self-love, and discontent with the portion which has been given to thee." All these are qualities not of the act but of the one who does it, and the matter is decided within, in the ruling faculty.

"Thou seest how few the things are." Marcus does not demand heroism of himself: a few things are enough to "live a life which flows in quiet, and is like the existence of the gods." The Greek is εὔρουν βίον, a life that "flows well," and Long's "flows in quiet" keeps the image. With this very word Zeno, the founder of the school, defined happiness as "a good flow of life." Happiness for the Stoics, then, means not rapture but a course without obstructions, and it is arranged not somewhere in the future but in each task that is in one's hands now.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^virtue-is-sufficient|Virtue is sufficient for happiness]], and so the things required are few. [[app-d-map-of-practice#^self-is-hegemonikon|The true self is the ruling faculty]], and everything is decided within, in how the act is done. And from discontent with one's portion one is freed by [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|acceptance of fate]].

## 2.14

![[02-14#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

One can lose only what one has. The past is gone, the future is not yet, and one thing remains: the moment in which everything happens. "For what a man has not, how can any one take this from him?"

It follows that the longest life is no different from the shortest. "No man loses any other life than this which he now lives, nor lives any other than this which he now loses." The one who has lived eighty years and the one who died at thirty lose exactly the same thing, the present moment. Unlived years cannot be taken away: they lie in the future, which does not exist, and were never ours. The key word is τὸ παρόν, "the present," "what is at hand."

In the second half of the passage Marcus tells himself to keep two things in mind. The first is "that all things from eternity are of like forms and come round in a circle," and that it makes no difference whether one sees the same things "during a hundred years or two hundred, or an infinite time." The second repeats what has been said: "the longest liver and he who will die soonest lose just the same." Both are arguments against greed for time: if nothing new lies ahead, extra years give nothing that is not in this moment.

Life is happening right now and nowhere else. That is why fear, too, is directed at what is not: at what has not yet happened and what has already gone. Seneca says the same: "There are more things, Lucilius, likely to frighten us than there are to crush us; we suffer more often in imagination than in reality" (Letters 13.4, trans. Gummere). Where Gummere has "imagination," the Latin has *opinio*, "opinion."

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^eternal-recurrence|Everything comes round again]]: the world passes through the same forms. And a short life can be accepted calmly, because it is no poorer than a long one: in this lies [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|acceptance of fate]].

## 2.17

![[02-17#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

The first sentence consists of seven short definitions: "Of human life the time is a point, and the substance is in a flux, and the perception dull, and the composition of the whole body subject to putrefaction, and the soul a whirl, and fortune hard to divine, and fame a thing devoid of judgement."

One of these definitions deserves particular attention. The Greek is ψυχὴ ῥόμβος, and Long's "the soul a whirl" is close to it. Ῥόμβος means something that spins, a top or a whirl, and translators render it in different ways. The image is more exact than any adjective: the soul is not merely unstable; it spins without finding rest. Two more images follow: "everything which belongs to the body is a stream, and what belongs to the soul is a dream and vapour." And very briefly: "life is a warfare and a stranger's sojourn." The Greek πόλεμος is war, and Long keeps it.

The question follows: "What then is that which is able to conduct a man?" The Greek verb παραπέμπειν means "to escort, to see on one's way": so one escorts a traveler or gives him a guide for the road. Long's "conduct" keeps this sense. If life is a sojourn in a foreign land, one needs a companion on the way. The answer is: "One thing and only one, philosophy."

To philosophize is to keep "the daemon within a man free from violence and unharmed," "superior to pains and pleasures," to do "nothing without purpose, nor yet falsely and with hypocrisy," not to need "another man's doing or not doing anything," to accept all that happens "as coming from thence, wherever it is, from whence he himself came," and to wait for death "with a cheerful mind."

The last argument concerns death, which is only "a dissolution of the elements of which every living being is compounded." The elements constantly change into one another, and there is no harm to them in it; why then should we fear? "For it is according to nature, and nothing is evil which is according to nature."

The book ends with the note "This in Carnuntum." Carnuntum was a Roman camp on the Danube, Marcus's headquarters in the war against the Germanic tribes.

**Doctrines.** Several are gathered here at once. [[app-d-map-of-practice#^fame-is-empty|Fame is empty]]. [[app-d-map-of-practice#^eternal-recurrence|Everything flows and comes round again]]. Philosophy, the guide on this journey, teaches one to [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|live according to nature]]. And it teaches one to [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|accept all that happens, death included]], as coming from the same place from which one came oneself: what accords with nature cannot be evil.

## The Book in Brief

**Key thought.** Book II sets out the elements of the exercises to which Marcus will return later. Its key passages converge on one point: only the ruling faculty and only the present moment are in our power. Of flesh, breath and the ruling faculty, only the third does he count as truly his own ([[02-02|2.2]]). One can lose only the present ([[02-14|2.14]]), and the guide through a life that is "a warfare and a stranger's sojourn" proves to be philosophy alone ([[02-17|2.17]]).

**Practice: the anticipation of evils (praemeditatio malorum).** Marcus begins the morning by saying to himself: "I shall meet with the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial" ([[02-01|2.1]]). The exercise is to picture, before the day begins, the difficulties and unpleasant people one will meet in it, not in general but specifically, and to decide in advance how to regard them: what here is in our power and what is not, what is evil and what is not. Then the event meets a judgment already prepared and does not take one by surprise. Yet Marcus is not preparing to defend himself: the one who errs remains "akin to me," and to be vexed with him or turn away from him is to go against nature.
