### A Book Not Meant to Be Read

When we open a book, we usually assume that the author has put some thought into it, and that our task is simply to find it and take it with us. The *Meditations* of Marcus Aurelius do not yield to this approach, and not because the text is difficult. On the contrary, it is easy: the passages are short, the words are plain, and there is almost no ornament. That very ease is misleading. A reader who goes through the book from cover to cover comes away with a handful of elegant sayings and believes he has taken everything it had to give.

That is how I read it the first time. What I had before me was a scatter of notes, unconnected and repeating the same few things: everything flows, everything dies, do not be angry with people, do your work. I concluded that I was holding the fragments of some lost treatise, and I kept asking myself **what was wrong with this text**. The question I should have asked was a different one: **why did Marcus write this at all**.

He seems to give the answer himself. In [[03-14|Book III]] he calls his notes τὰ ὑπομνημάτιά σου, "your little notes." The word comes from a verb meaning "to remind." George Long renders it as "thy own memoirs." But in ancient usage ὑπομνήματα (*hypomnemata*) were reminders, notes and drafts: writing a person does for his own use. A memoir is written for others; these notes were not. Marcus wrote for himself and had no thought of showing what he wrote, still less of publishing it. **The reader here is an outsider**, and whoever forgets this reads someone else's notes as though they were addressed to him.

For Marcus the act of writing was itself an exercise. He wrote a thought down not to preserve it but so that it would act on his anger, his fear, his weariness and his vanity. Each passage is an effort to bring his own mind back to the state his philosophy required of it.

### Spiritual Exercises

This activity has a name, and it was Pierre Hadot (1922–2010), the French historian of ancient philosophy, who restored it to use. He showed that ancient philosophy was less a body of doctrine than a **way of life**, and that many of its texts were written not to convey a theory but to transform the one who wrote them. The practices by which a philosopher worked on himself Hadot called **spiritual exercises**[^1], and to Marcus he devoted a whole book, *The Inner Citadel*[^2].

What these exercises were can be seen from a list given by Philo of Alexandria, the Jewish philosopher of the first century: inquiry, reading, listening, attention, self-mastery, meditation, the therapy of the passions, the remembrance of good things, indifference to indifferent things[^3]. None of these is an occasion for theoretical debate. Theory serves here only as a support, something that allows a person to choose a way of life and to hold to it.

This brings us to a distinction one should grasp before opening Marcus. Today a philosopher is a specialist who writes in a special language understood only by colleagues and competes with them in theories. In antiquity a philosopher was someone who **lived** philosophically. His contemporaries regarded Marcus as a philosopher, though he left not a single treatise, and he put it briefly himself in [[10-16|Book X]]:

> No longer talk at all about the kind of man that a good man ought to be, but be such.

### What Marcus Repeats

The *Meditations*, then, consist of spiritual exercises. But what exactly is Marcus exercising? The answer is simple: the basic principles of the Stoic school, to which he returns again and again. The Stoics called them **doctrines** (δόγματα) in a technical sense: a principle proved in theory and folded into a single sentence, so that it could be used at the moment when there is no time to reason. There are not many of them. I count fourteen doctrines in the *Meditations*. For example: the good lies only in one's own virtue; things do not touch us, only our judgment of them does; the one who does wrong harms himself, not me; men are born for one another; everything comes round again; what the whole assigns must be accepted.

This also explains the chief "oddity" of the book, its repetitions and its going in circles. Marcus explains them himself in [[05-09|Book V]]: one should "not return to philosophy as if she were a master, but act like those who have sore eyes and apply a bit of sponge and egg." A remedy is taken each time the eye becomes inflamed again, and each repetition in the book is another dose of the philosophical remedy. In [[07-02|Book VII]] he explains why the dose is needed again and again: principles die when the impressions that correspond to them go out, but "it is in thy power continuously to fan these thoughts into a flame." The Greek verb is ἀναζωπυρεῖν, to blow on coals until they glow.

It follows that only someone who **does what Marcus did**, who practices himself, can get real use from this book. It is not enough to read it and be inspired by fine quotations. One has to choose the principles that concern oneself, keep them at hand, and return to them again and again, at the moment they are needed.

### The Heart of Stoicism

All of Stoicism can be reduced to a single thought: a person should seek only the good he is able to attain, and avoid only the evil he is able to avoid. Since such good and such evil depend solely on a person's freedom, they are moral good and moral evil.

All things, then, fall into two kinds. To the first belongs what depends on us: our judgments and our desires. To the second belongs everything else: health, other people's opinions, money, success, death. Trouble begins when a person places his happiness in things of the second kind, for then any accident can take it from him. Marcus says this in [[08-47|Book VIII]]:

> If thou art pained by any external thing, it is not this thing that disturbs thee, but thy own judgement about it. And it is in thy power to wipe out this judgement now.

The state to which this exercise leads the Stoics called *apatheia* (**ἀπάθεια**), freedom from the passions. It should not be mistaken for coldness or insensibility: by a passion the Stoics meant not a feeling as such but a false judgment that has taken hold of a person. Marcus himself rarely uses the word. More often he speaks of calm, of the smooth flow of life, of the quiet harbor a sailor finds when, "like a mariner who has doubled the promontory," he reaches "a waveless bay" ([[12-22|12.22]]). The aim in every case is the same: to recover command of one's own life where that command truly exists.

### Always at Hand

How are doctrines to be kept ready? The Stoics said they should be **πρόχειρα**, "at hand," and Marcus writes about this in [[04-03|Book IV]]:

> …let thy principles be brief and fundamental, which, as soon as thou shalt recur to them, will be sufficient to cleanse the soul completely, and to send thee back free from all discontent with the things to which thou returnest.

These principles must be brief enough to fit into a single breath, and they must work at once, before the passion does. The *Meditations* are a collection of such remedies, meant to be carried at all times. Perhaps Marcus wrote them on wax tablets; in any case, the notes after the first and second books ("Among the Quadi at the Granua," "This in Carnuntum") show that some of the passages were written far from home, on campaign and in military camps.

### Everything Rests on the Present Moment

The foundation of a philosopher's whole life is the present moment in which we live. A doctrine kept at hand is useless if one fails to notice the moment when it must be applied. That is why the central thing in Stoic practice turns out to be **προσοχή**, attention: a constant watchfulness over what is happening now, what I feel, what I judge, how I act.

The Stoic lives in the present for the simple reason that there is nowhere else to live. Marcus says so in [[02-14|Book II]]:

> For the present is the same to all, though that which perishes is not the same; and so that which is lost appears to be a mere moment. For a man cannot lose either the past or the future: for what a man has not, how can any one take this from him?

### How to Read This Book

Hence a few rules worth keeping in mind when reading the *Meditations*:

- **Read slowly.** Not straight through like a novel, but one or two passages at a time. Find the doctrine in the passage and state it in your own words.
- **Remember the genre.** The *Meditations* are neither a treatise nor a diary but *hypomnemata*, notes to oneself. What you have before you is not a confession or a record of pessimism, but exercises.
- **Take what is yours.** Do not try to seize everything at once. Find two or three principles that answer to your own circumstances, write them down briefly, and return to them whenever you need them.

This book is neither a retelling nor a biography of Marcus Aurelius; it is meant as a companion for reading the *Meditations*. It contains their whole text in the translation of George Long, in order, book by book. Each of the twelve books of the *Meditations* has a chapter, and all the chapters are built the same way. Each begins with "Line of the Book," which says what that book of the *Meditations* is about and which passages in it matter most. An introduction follows, listing the book's themes with passage numbers and pointing out a few places worth noticing. Then comes the text itself. Many passages are left without commentary; a detailed commentary on every one of them can be found at [readaurelius.org](https://readaurelius.org). About a hundred key passages are discussed in the book itself: what the Greek says, where the translation loses the sense, which other passages it connects with, and which doctrine is at work in it. Each chapter ends with "The Book in Brief": the key thought of that book and one practice that Marcus himself performs in it.

The book closes with five appendices: on the text and the translation, a glossary of terms, an index of names, a map of practice that lets you find every place where Marcus returns to the same doctrine or exercise, and a chronology of his life. You can read it straight through, one chapter at a time, or start from the map of practice and go to the doctrine you need.
