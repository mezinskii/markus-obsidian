## Line of the Book

**Bringing Everything Down to a Few Things.** Almost everything said before returns here in its most compressed form. And the book ends with a farewell addressed, it seems, no longer to himself but to man in general (12.36).

Nine key passages: everything can be had now (12.1) → three parts and the sphere of Empedocles (12.3) → in what condition death should find you (12.7) → questions to put to things (12.8) → doubling the promontory (12.22) → life as a sum of acts (12.23) → three principles in readiness (12.24) → what you have forgotten (12.26) → depart in peace (12.36).

## Introduction

Book XII is the last, and its formulas are very short: "everything is opinion" (12.8, 12.22). "Cast away opinion: thou art saved. Who then hinders thee from casting it away?" (12.25).

Here too Marcus Aurelius explains most precisely how doctrines are to be kept at hand. "In the application of thy principles thou must be like the pancratiast, not like the gladiator; for the gladiator lets fall the sword which he uses and is killed; but the other always has his hand, and needs to do nothing else than use it" (12.9). The pancratiast fought without weapons, with hands alone. The Greek verb for what happens to the gladiator, ἀναιρεῖται, can be read two ways: Long takes it as "is killed"; read otherwise, it means that the gladiator puts his sword down and takes it up again. Either way the point is the same: a doctrine one must reach for may not be found at the moment it is needed, while a doctrine that has become one's hand is always there. This is the "at hand," πρόχειρα, spoken of in the book's introduction and in the practice of Book XI.

The passages fall into several themes.

- **The present and time:** 12.1, 12.3, 12.7, 12.21, 12.31, 12.32.
- **Everything is opinion:** 12.8, 12.22, 12.25, 12.29, 12.33.
- **At hand:** 12.9, 12.24, 12.26.
- **The gods and providence:** 12.5, 12.12, 12.14, 12.28.
- **The ruling faculty:** 12.2, 12.4, 12.11, 12.19, 12.30.
- **Analysis of things:** 12.10, 12.18, 12.20.
- **Death and departure:** 12.15, 12.23, 12.27, 12.34, 12.35, 12.36.

Among the passages without commentary, 12.15 deserves notice: "Does the light of the lamp shine without losing its splendour until it is extinguished; and shall the truth which is in thee and justice and temperance be extinguished before thy death?"

## 12.1

![[12-01#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

The last book opens with a promise: "All those things at which thou wishest to arrive by a circuitous road, thou canst have now," and a condition: "if thou dost not refuse them to thyself." The Greek is sharper: ἐὰν μὴ σαυτῷ φθονῇς, "if thou dost not begrudge thyself," that is, if you do not grudge yourself your own good. The only obstacle is the man himself.

Then comes the formula of the three times: "take no notice of all the past, and trust the future to providence, and direct the present only conformably to piety and justice." The Greek ἀπευθύνῃς means "to straighten," as one straightens what is crooked, and Long's "direct" keeps something of this. Nothing can be done with the past, the future is not in your keeping, and only the present can be set straight.

Piety is needed "that thou mayest be content with the lot which is assigned to thee, for nature designed it for thee and thee for it"; justice, "that thou mayest always speak the truth freely and without disguise, and do the things which are agreeable to law and according to the worth of each." In other words: accept what does not depend on you, and act rightly in what does. As for the sensations of the body, let them stay with the body: "the passive part will look to this."

The passage ends thus: fear "not because thou must some time cease to live," but "never to have begun to live according to nature." Then you will "cease to be a stranger in thy native land," that is, in the world, and cease to wonder at each day as at something unexpected.

**Doctrines.** Piety consists in being content with the lot for which nature designed you: this is [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|acceptance of fate]]. What is to be feared is not dying but never having begun to [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|live according to nature]].

## 12.3

![[12-03#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

The theme is familiar; it first appeared in [[02-02|2.2]]: "The things are three of which thou art composed, a little body, a little breath (life), intelligence." Long keeps the Greek diminutives σωμάτιον and πνευμάτιον, "a little body, a little breath." But here an important qualification is made: the first two are yours "so far as it is thy duty to take care of them." The body is not despised: it is yours in the measure in which you are responsible for it, and "the third alone is properly thine."

Marcus then lists what must be separated from the mind: what others do or say, what you yourself have done or said, the trouble about the future, whatever is attached to you "independent of thy will," and "the external circumfluent vortex." The most unexpected item is the second: one's own past must be separated too. The sentence grows long and loses its way, and he begins it again: "if thou wilt separate, I say..."

The mind so separated will live free, "doing what is just and accepting what happens and saying the truth." Action, desire, assent: again the three disciplines, this time in three phrases. Marcus then borrows an image from Empedocles: "All round, and in its joyous rest reposing." We have already met the soul as a sphere, the perfect form, in [[11-12|11.12]].

The end of the passage: to pass the rest of life "free from perturbations, nobly, and obedient to thy own daemon." Here Long departs from the Greek. "Nobly" stands for εὐμενῶς, "kindly," and "obedient" for ἵλεως, "gracious, at peace with," a word from the language of prayer. With this same word the *Meditations* will end in [[12-36|12.36]], but in Long the echo is lost.

**Doctrines.** The body and the breath are yours only in part, and the past and the doings of others are [[app-d-map-of-practice#^dichotomy-of-control|not in your power]]; only the third part is truly yours, because [[app-d-map-of-practice#^self-is-hegemonikon|the true self is the ruling faculty]]. The separated mind accepts what happens, and in this lies [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|acceptance of fate]].

## 12.7

![[12-07#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

In the Greek there is not even a verb "consider" here; Long adds it twice. Four objects stand in a row, and this is not an argument but a list to which one returns. And the first item is not "when you will die" but in what condition death should find you: it is the only thing here that depends on you.

"The boundless abyss of time" is in Greek ἀχάνεια, from a verb meaning "to gape," "to open the mouth wide": time opens like jaws in both directions, "past and future." The order of the four objects runs from within outward: my condition at the hour of death, the shortness of my life, the abyss of time, "the feebleness of all matter."

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|Death is a work of nature]], and there is no point in quarreling with it; what matters is only the condition in which it finds you.

## 12.8

![[12-08#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

The causes of things are to be seen "bare of their coverings," and in actions one looks at their purposes. Then four things are named around which ordinary life turns: pain, pleasure, death, fame; two attract and two repel. Marcus does not assess them but puts a question to each: what is it? Definition is the remedy, the stripped view of [[06-13|6.13]] and [[08-11|8.11]].

Then he turns to himself: "who is to himself the cause of his uneasiness," and "how no man is hindered by another." The last item, on which all the rest depend, reads: "everything is opinion." The same formula will open [[12-22|12.22]].

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^fame-is-empty|Fame is empty]], as is seen once its covering is removed. Each man is the cause of his own uneasiness, because everything is opinion, and opinion is [[app-d-map-of-practice#^dichotomy-of-control|in our power]].

## 12.22

![[12-22#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

The first half of the sentence is familiar: it closes the list in [[12-08|12.8]]. But here the main thing is added: "and opinion is in thy power." Without this addition the words "everything is opinion" would be mere theory; with it the theory becomes a tool, because judgment is the only thing wholly in your power.

The verb is the same as in [[04-07|4.7]], and Long keeps it in both places: ἆρον, "take away." Not "refute," not "argue yourself out of it," but take away, as one takes off what has been laid on top. And "when thou choosest": there is nothing to wait for.

An image follows: "like a mariner who has doubled the promontory." The Greek ἄκρα is precisely a promontory, a cape. Along an open coast the sea is rough; once the cape is rounded, the sailor is at once in calm water. What matters most is that the cape has not tamed the sea: the storm has not died down, the cape has only come between the sailor and the wave. So with opinion: the world remains hard and loud, but its waves no longer reach you through judgment.

There is a subtlety in the Greek that the translation does not convey. Long writes "thou wilt find calm," with the verb in the future, as a reward for effort. In Marcus there is no verb at this point at all: γαλήνη, σταθερὰ πάντα, κόλπος ἀκύμων, "calm, everything stable, a waveless bay." The calm, then, is not a reward that will come later but what sets in at once, as soon as the cape is rounded.

**Doctrines.** The sea is not in our power, but [[app-d-map-of-practice#^dichotomy-of-control|opinion is in our power]], and so the cape can be rounded whenever one chooses.

## 12.23

![[12-23#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

The argument is built as a syllogism. The premise: "Any one activity, whatever it may be, when it has ceased at its proper time, suffers no evil because it has ceased," nor does the one who was engaged in it. Then a definition: life is "the whole, which consists of all the acts." It follows that its ending at the proper time is no evil either.

The formulation is careful. Marcus does not say that death is no evil at all; he proves only one thing: that it suffers no evil "for this reason, that it has ceased."

Who sets the term? Nature: sometimes a man's own, as in old age, but always the universal nature, "by the change of whose parts the whole universe continues ever young and perfect." The death of the parts is what keeps the world young.

So the end of life is no evil, because it is not shameful, "since it is both independent of the will and not opposed to the general interest." More than that, it is good, "since it is seasonable, and profitable to and congruent with the universal." The last sentence: "For thus too he is moved by the Deity who is moved in the same manner with the Deity." The Greek has one word, θεοφόρητος, "borne by a god," which was said of a prophet in ecstasy. Marcus keeps from this word not the ecstasy but the direction: borne by God is the one who moves where God moves.

**Doctrines.** Death in its season is useful to the whole, and to accept it is to come to [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|acceptance of fate]]. [[app-d-map-of-practice#^unity-of-cosmos|All things are bound into one whole]], and the change of the parts keeps the world young.

## 12.24

![[12-24#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

The first principle concerns actions: do nothing "either inconsiderately or otherwise than as justice herself would act." The Greek has Δίκη, Justice as a goddess, and Long keeps the personification with "herself": the measure is not a rule but the way she acts. As for what comes from outside, remember that it happens "either by chance or according to providence," and "thou must neither blame chance nor accuse providence." This is the dilemma of [[06-10|6.10]] ("either a confusion... or it is unity and order and providence") in its shortest form.

The second principle: trace the life of every being "from the seed to the time of its receiving a soul, and from the reception of a soul to the giving back of the same," and see of what it is compounded and into what it is resolved. The Greek ἀποδοῦναι, "giving back," is what one does with a debt, and Long keeps it: this is the thought of breath held on loan from [[06-15|6.15]].

The third principle is the view from above. Raised above the earth, you will see human things, and however often you are raised up, you will see the same: "sameness of form and shortness of duration." The last sentence reads: "Are these things to be proud of?" The Greek has τῦφος, literally "smoke," figuratively "conceit," "puffed-up pride"; it was the word by which the Cynics named human self-importance.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^providence|There is nothing to complain of in the order of the world]]: one cannot accuse providence, and if it is chance, it is pointless to blame it.

## 12.26

![[12-26#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

The passage is built on a single verb: "thou hast forgotten." "When thou art troubled about anything, thou hast forgotten this," and then comes a list of what has been forgotten. Distress is not refuted here; it is declared a sign of forgetfulness.

The list is an almost complete summary of what we have seen throughout the *Meditations*. All things happen according to the universal nature. Another's wrongful act is nothing to you. Everything that happens always happened so and will happen so. All men are kin, "not of a little blood or seed, but of intelligence." Every man's intelligence "is a god and is an efflux of the Deity." "Nothing is a man's own, but... his child and his body and his very soul came from the Deity." Everything is opinion. And each lives only the present and can lose only that.

The Greek speaks of blood kinship in deliberately diminutive words, αἱμάτιον and σπερμάτιον, and Long's "a little blood or seed" conveys them. Kinship by reason is wider than kinship by blood, and so the human race is one without exception. This repeats [[02-01|2.1]], the beginning of Book II. The same diminutives return a line later, "the little child, the little body, the little soul," but here Long drops them.

The last item seems to remove the ground for distress altogether: one can lose only the present, and that is not much.

**Doctrines.** The man who is troubled has forgotten that [[app-d-map-of-practice#^eternal-recurrence|everything comes round again]] and that [[app-d-map-of-practice#^oikeiosis|men are kin by reason]]: it makes little sense to be angry with a kinsman for his error. And [[app-d-map-of-practice#^externals-on-loan|nothing is our own]]: even the child, the body and the soul are given for a time.

## 12.36

![[12-36#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

"Man, thou hast been a citizen in this great state": so begins the last passage of the *Meditations*. The whole book Marcus wrote "to himself," but this passage, it seems, is addressed to man in general. The world, as in [[06-44|6.44]], is called a state, and the measure is a civic one: "that which is conformable to the laws is just for all." Five years or three, it makes no difference.

Who sends you away from this city? "No tyrant nor yet an unjust judge," but "nature, who brought thee into it." The one who brought you in leads you out; there is neither caprice nor judicial error here, and nothing to resent.

A praetor who has employed an actor dismisses him from the stage. The actor objects: "But I have not finished the five acts, but only three of them." Marcus agrees: "Thou sayest well, but in life the three acts are the whole drama." It is not the actor who decides when the play is complete, but "him who was once the cause of its composition, and now of its dissolution." Long is clear here: "what shall be a complete drama is determined by him," for the Greek τὸ τέλειον ὁρίζει. This is the same thought as in [[11-01|11.1]]: the life of the rational soul is complete at any moment, wherever it is cut short.

The last words: "Depart then satisfied, for he also who releases thee is satisfied." Long keeps the repetition, and it matters: the Greek has the same word twice, ἵλεως, "gracious," which also stood at the end of [[12-03|12.3]]. Long's "satisfied" is cooler than the Greek, which speaks of kindness rather than contentment. The *Meditations* end, then, not with a command and not with a consolation, but with reciprocity: depart in peace, because the one who releases you releases you in peace.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^cosmopolis|The world is a single city]], and the term of life in it is set by the nature that brought one into it.

## The Book in Brief

**Key thought.** Everything sought by a circuitous road can be had now: leave the past, trust the future to providence, and set the present straight ([[12-01|12.1]]). Only opinion stands in the way, and opinion is in our power: take it away, and you find yourself in calm water, like a mariner who has doubled the promontory ([[12-22|12.22]]). And one should depart from life as one is released from it: graciously ([[12-36|12.36]]).

**Practice: attention (προσοχή).** Marcus gives the formula in the first passage of the book: everything one desires can be had now, "if thou wilt take no notice of all the past, and trust the future to providence, and direct the present only conformably to piety and justice" ([[12-01|12.1]]). The exercise is to narrow each task, before beginning it, to what is in one's hands now, to ask briefly whether it is mine or not mine, and during the work not to wander off in thought into plans, grievances and daydreams. The point is not to be attentive always but to return to attention each time one notices that one has strayed. Marcus connects this with the end of life: "if thou shalt strive to live only what is really thy life, that is, the present," then "thou wilt be able to pass that portion of life which remains for thee up to the time of thy death, free from perturbations" ([[12-03|12.3]]).
