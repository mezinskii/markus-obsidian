## Line of the Book

**Living Among Men.** Book IX opens with a charge of impiety: to act unjustly toward men is to sin against nature and the gods (9.1). It closes with a long argument against anger at the shameless (9.42). Between the two runs a single theme: how to live and act among people who think otherwise, expecting from them neither agreement nor gratitude.

Eight key passages: injustice as impiety (9.1) → wait for death as one waits for a birth (9.3) → three things that are enough (9.6) → four commands (9.7) → if there is a god, all is well (9.28) → do not expect Plato's Republic (9.29) → ample space (9.32) → five remedies against anger (9.42).

## Introduction

In 9.11 Marcus Aurelius gives a rule in two sentences: "If thou art able, correct by teaching those who do wrong; but if thou canst not, remember that indulgence is given to thee for this purpose." In 9.24 and 9.30 he looks at men from above, and in 9.3 he admits how heavy he finds "the discordance of those who live together."

The passages fall into several themes.

- **Justice and impiety:** 9.1, 9.4, 9.5, 9.16, 9.23.
- **The faults of others:** 9.11, 9.18, 9.20, 9.27, 9.34, 9.38, 9.42.
- **Judgment and the three things that are enough:** 9.6, 9.7, 9.13, 9.15, 9.31, 9.32.
- **The whole, providence or atoms:** 9.8, 9.9, 9.10, 9.28, 9.39, 9.40.
- **Flux and death:** 9.3, 9.14, 9.19, 9.21, 9.33, 9.35, 9.36.
- **The view from above:** 9.24, 9.30.
- **Doing one's own work:** 9.2, 9.12, 9.17, 9.22, 9.25, 9.26, 9.29, 9.37.

Among the passages without commentary, 9.40, on prayer, deserves notice. If the gods have power, Marcus asks, "why dost thou not pray for them to give thee the faculty of not fearing any of the things which thou fearest, or of not desiring any of the things which thou desirest, or not being pained at anything, rather than pray that any of these things should not happen or happen?"

## 9.1

![[09-01#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

Book IX opens with the sentence "He who acts unjustly acts impiously." Injustice, then, is not only an offense against men but a sin against the divine. Why? Because "the universal nature has made rational animals for the sake of one another to help one another according to their deserts, but in no way to injure one another." Whoever harms a man transgresses "her will," and nature for the Stoic is itself a divinity. Long calls it "the highest divinity"; the Greek says "the oldest of the gods," πρεσβυτάτη τῶν θεῶν.

Marcus then widens the circle. The liar too is impious, since the same nature "is named truth." Not only the one who lies intentionally, but also the one who lies unintentionally: nature gave him "powers" to tell the true from the false, and through their neglect he can no longer do so.

Impious too is the man who "pursues pleasure as good, and avoids pain as evil." Such a man must often find fault with nature because the bad enjoy pleasure and the good have pain for their share, and complaint against the order of the world is itself impiety.

The conclusion follows. Pain and pleasure, death and life, honor and dishonor are things "which the universal nature employs equally"; the Greek ἐπίσης means "evenly, alike." Whoever wishes to follow nature must be "equally affected" toward them as well. What the Stoics call indifference to indifferent things here becomes piety: to treat unevenly what nature treats evenly is to quarrel with God.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^oikeiosis|Men are born for one another]], and that is why injustice is impiety. [[app-d-map-of-practice#^providence|The world is ordered by providence]]. Whoever wishes to [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|live according to nature]] treats evenly what nature treats evenly.

## 9.3

![[09-03#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

"Do not despise death, but be well content with it, since this too is one of those things which nature wills." Contempt for death would still be a pose; Marcus asks for something quieter, to be "neither careless nor impatient nor contemptuous with respect to death."

He places death among the other works of nature: "to be young and to grow old, and to increase and to reach maturity, and to have teeth and beard and grey hairs, and to beget, and to be pregnant and to bring forth." Dissolution belongs to the same order as everything else in the life of the body. Hence the comparison: "As thou now waitest for the time when the child shall come out of thy wife's womb, so be ready for the time when thy soul shall fall out of this envelope." The word "now" matters: when Marcus wrote this, Faustina was apparently expecting a child. Birth and death are described as one and the same crossing.

Then comes what Long calls "a vulgar kind of comfort"; the Greek ἰδιωτικός means plain, the comfort of an ordinary man rather than of a philosopher. It is to look at what one is leaving and at the people one is leaving. One must not be offended with men; it is "thy duty to care for them and to bear with them gently." And yet one should remember that the parting "will not be from men who have the same principles as thyself." The only thing that could hold us to life would be "to be permitted to live with those who have the same principles as ourselves," and that is not granted. At the end comes an almost bitter cry: "Come quick, O death, lest perchance I, too, should forget myself." A long life among people who think otherwise is a danger: one may forget one's own principles.

**Doctrines.** Death belongs with growth, grey hair and childbirth, and to wait for it calmly, as one waits for a birth, is [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|acceptance of fate]]. This is how one who [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|lives according to nature]] meets death. And [[app-d-map-of-practice#^oikeiosis|men are born for one another]], so one must care even for those who do not agree.

## 9.6

![[09-06#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

All three disciplines are named here at once. In the Greek the chief word stands first: ἀρκεῖ, "it is enough." Long moves it to the end, "that is enough," where it sounds like a conclusion. Marcus lists three things and adds nothing more: neither wealth, nor health, nor anyone's approval. These three suffice.

Sound "opinion founded on understanding" means not accepting unexamined the valuation that comes along with an impression, and separating the fact from what is attached to it ([[04-07|4.7]]). "Conduct directed to social good" requires remembering that "we are made for co-operation, like feet, like hands, like eyelids" ([[02-01|2.1]]). "Contentment with everything which happens" means accepting what befalls us as the stones in a wall fit one another in [[05-08|5.8]]. Assent, action and desire are the same three conditions we saw in [[08-07|8.7]], here pressed into a single line.

One more word matters, and Long keeps it: "thy present," three times over, for the Greek παροῦσα. The opinion is about what is before one's eyes, the conduct is what one is doing now, the contentment is with what is happening.

**Doctrines.** The word "enough" is itself a doctrine: [[app-d-map-of-practice#^virtue-is-sufficient|virtue is sufficient for happiness]], that is, sound judgment, the common work and acceptance. Contentment with everything that comes from outside is [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|acceptance of fate]].

## 9.7

![[09-07#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

The first two of the four commands repeat almost word for word the opening of the seven commands in [[07-29|7.29]]: the same verb ἐξαλεῖψαι, "wipe out," the same στῆσαι, "stop." Long keeps the first ("Wipe out the imagination" there, "Wipe out imagination" here), but renders the second differently: "Stop the pulling of the strings" in 7.29, "check desire" here.

This passage is the counterpart of [[09-06|9.6]]. There the three things were given as what must be done: judge rightly, act for the common good, accept. Here they are given as what must be cut short: wipe out the impression, check the impulse, extinguish the appetite (the Greek σβέσαι is used of putting out a fire). The last command, "keep the ruling faculty in its own power," says what all of this is for.

The order matters: first the impression, then the impulse, then the appetite. Impulse and appetite are born of an impression that has been accepted, and if the impression is wiped out at the start, they have nothing to be born from.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^self-is-hegemonikon|The true self is the ruling faculty]], and everything depends on its remaining in its own power.

## 9.28

![[09-28#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

"The periodic movements of the universe are the same, up and down from age to age." Then follows a choice between three branches, as more than once in Marcus. Either the universal intelligence "puts itself in motion for every separate effect," and then be content with what it brings about; or it moved once, and everything else follows by sequence; or "indivisible elements are the origin of all things."

The conclusion fits in one line: "if there is a god, all is well; and if chance rules, do not thou also be governed by it." The first two branches both speak of a rational order of the world, so the conclusion comes back to the dilemma "providence or atoms" of [[04-03|4.3]] and [[06-10|6.10]], in its shortest form. If the world is ordered by God, trust it; if by chance, do not be random yourself. That is what the Greek says, μὴ καὶ σὺ εἰκῇ, "do not thou also act at random"; Long's "be governed by it" makes chance a ruler, which the Greek does not.

The second half is about flux. "Soon will the earth cover us all: then the earth, too, will change," and so on without end. Whoever reflects on "the changes and transformations which follow one another like wave after wave and their rapidity" will despise everything perishable. Long's "wave after wave" renders a single Greek word, ἐπικυματώσεις, the surging of waves one upon another.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^providence|The world is ordered by providence]]. And if it is chance, chance must not rule a man's reason.

## 9.29

![[09-29#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

The passage begins with flux: "The universal cause is like a winter torrent: it carries everything along with it." The Greek χειμάρρους is a stream that fills only with the winter rains and sweeps away whatever lies in its bed. Against this background the "poor people who are engaged in matters political, and, as they suppose, are playing the philosopher" look pitiful. "All drivellers," says Long; the Greek μυξῶν μεστά is cruder still, "full of snot."

"Well then, man: do what nature now requires." Act if it is in your power, "and do not look about thee to see if any one will observe it." "Nor yet expect Plato's Republic: but be content if the smallest thing goes on well." The ideal state of Plato's book cannot be built. "For who can change men's opinions?" And without that there is nothing but "the slavery of men who groan while they pretend to obey." Marcus understands that no decree can make men philosophers.

Then he tests the great examples: Alexander, his father Philip, and Demetrius of Phalerum, a philosopher who governed Athens for about ten years. If they discovered what the common nature required, well and good. "But if they acted like tragedy heroes, no one has condemned me to imitate them." And the conclusion: "Simple and modest is the work of philosophy. Draw me not aside to insolence and pride."

**Doctrines.** To [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|live according to nature]] is to "do what nature now requires" and to "be content if the smallest thing goes on well." And [[app-d-map-of-practice#^fame-is-empty|fame is empty]]: the work of philosophy is "simple and modest" and needs no spectators.

**Commentary.**

The advice comes in two steps. The first: "Thou canst remove out of the way many useless things among those which disturb thee, for they lie entirely in thy opinion." Much of what weighs on us lies, then, not in things but in judgment.

The second explains how to do it: "thou wilt then gain for thyself ample space by comprehending the whole universe in thy mind." The Greek εὐρυχωρία, "ample space," means literally "a broad place." Think of the eternity of time, of how quickly each thing changes, of how short the span from birth to dissolution, and of "the illimitable time before birth as well as the equally boundless time after dissolution."

This is the contemplation of nature used as a remedy. What weighs on us becomes small not because it is denied, but because the whole of eternity appears beside it.

**Doctrines.** Much of what weighs on us lies in opinion, and [[app-d-map-of-practice#^dichotomy-of-control|opinion is in our power]], so it can be removed.

## 9.42

**Commentary.**

The book ends with its longest and most systematic passage: five remedies against anger.

The first remedy. When someone's shamelessness offends you, ask yourself: "Is it possible, then, that shameless men should not be in the world? It is not possible. Do not, then, require what is impossible." This is the fig tree of [[04-06|4.6]]: to demand that the shameless man not be shameless is to refuse to "allow the fig-tree to have juice." Remember this, and "thou wilt become more kindly disposed towards every one individually."

The second remedy. Against every wrong nature has given a virtue: "as an antidote against the stupid man, mildness." Long's "stupid" is too weak for the Greek ἀγνώμων, the unfeeling and ungrateful man. Against anger, then, we already have a remedy at hand, and it is always possible "to correct by teaching the man who is gone astray; for every man who errs misses his object."

The third remedy. "Besides wherein hast thou been injured?" None of those against whom you are irritated "has done anything by which thy mind could be made worse," and only there can evil and harm to you have their foundation.

The fourth remedy. "What harm is done or what is there strange, if the man who has not been instructed does the acts of an uninstructed man?" Perhaps you should blame yourself: your reason gave you every means to foresee this error, "and yet thou hast forgotten and art amazed that he has erred."

The fifth remedy, "most of all," as Marcus says. If you complain of ingratitude, "turn to thyself": perhaps you did good not for its own sake but for a return. "Just as if the eye demanded a recompense for seeing, or the feet for walking." Man is formed by nature "to acts of benevolence"; having done good, he has already fulfilled his purpose "and he gets what is his own." We met the same thought in [[05-06|5.6]]: the vine produces its grapes and "seeks for nothing more."

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^no-harm-to-virtuous|No harm can be done to the virtuous]]. As the eye is made for seeing, so man is made for doing good, and this is to [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|live according to nature]]. The reward for a good deed lies in the deed itself, for [[app-d-map-of-practice#^virtue-is-sufficient|virtue is sufficient for happiness]].

## The Book in Brief

**Key thought.** One can live among those who disagree without demanding their agreement and without losing oneself. Injustice toward them is impiety, because men are made for one another ([[09-01|9.1]]). For such a life three things are enough: sound judgment, action for the common good, and contentment with what comes from outside ([[09-06|9.6]]). And there is no reason to be angry with the shameless: the world cannot be without them, and they do no harm to the mind ([[09-42|9.42]]).

**Practice: the contemplation of nature (φυσιολογία).** Marcus looks at the world as unceasing motion: "The periodic movements of the universe are the same, up and down from age to age" ([[09-28|9.28]]). The exercise is to take what weighs on one, including another's fault or "the discordance of those who live together" ([[09-03|9.3]]), and to see it as one of the works of nature: how it arose, what it will pass into, how short its term is. Marcus applies this to himself as well.
