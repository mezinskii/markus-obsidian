## Line of the Book

**The Work of a Human Being.** Book V, like Book II, begins in the morning: Marcus Aurelius does not want to get up, and he reminds himself: "I am rising to the work of a human being" (5.1). The whole book answers the question of what that work is.

Seven key passages: rising for the work (5.1) → the prescriptions of Aesculapius (5.8) → returning to philosophy after a failure (5.9) → the soul is dyed by its thoughts (5.16) → the river of substance (5.23) → living with the gods (5.27) → smoke and the open door (5.29).

## Introduction

Book V has thirty-seven passages, fewer than Book IV. In Book IV Marcus spoke above all of transience: everything flows, fame is empty, death is natural. In Book V the chief word is "work." Man is made not for things to happen to him but to act, and to act for the common good.

The passages fall into several themes.

- **The work of a human being and the common good:** 5.1, 5.3, 5.6, 5.16, 5.20, 5.22, 5.30, 5.31, 5.36.
- **The gods and fate:** 5.7, 5.8, 5.18, 5.21, 5.24, 5.27.
- **Judgment:** things do not touch the soul: 5.2, 5.19, 5.26, 5.34, 5.35.
- **Difficult people:** 5.17, 5.25, 5.28.
- **Flux, death and freedom:** 5.4, 5.10, 5.13, 5.23, 5.29, 5.33.
- **What the good is:** 5.12, 5.14, 5.15, 5.37.
- **Self-examination and returning to philosophy:** 5.5, 5.9, 5.11, 5.32.

Two passages without commentary are worth marking. In 5.6 Marcus describes the man who does good and expects no thanks: "he is like a vine which has produced grapes, and seeks for nothing more after it has once produced its proper fruit." And in 5.28 he asks himself whether it is worth being angry with a man who smells: "Art thou angry with him whose armpits stink?" Here one sees that he can speak with humor of the people who irritate him.

## 5.1

![[05-01#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

In [[02-01|2.1]] Marcus prepared himself to meet "the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant"; here he has to argue with himself: he does not want to get up.

Laziness objects, and he answers it. "But this is more pleasant." "But it is necessary to take rest also." Marcus does not pretend that such thoughts do not exist; he hears them out seriously.

The first argument is that a man has his own work. "I am rising to the work of a human being," in Greek ἐπὶ ἀνθρώπου ἔργον. He tells himself to keep this sentence at hand (πρόχειρον ἔστω, which Long renders "let this thought be present"), so as not to argue with laziness empty-handed in the morning. A question follows: "Dost thou exist then to take thy pleasure, and not at all for action or exertion?" The Greek holds another question here: πρὸς πεῖσιν ἢ πρὸς ἐνέργειαν, were you made to have things happen to you, or to act? Long's "take thy pleasure" loses this contrast. An example follows: Marcus tells himself to look at "the little plants, the little birds, the ants, the spiders, the bees working together to put in order their several parts of the universe." Long keeps the Greek diminutives, φυτάρια, στρουθάρια. If the spider does not shirk its share, shall I?

The second argument is subtler. Rest is needed, Marcus agrees, and nature has fixed a measure for it, as for eating and drinking. Yet in rest, he tells himself, "thou goest beyond these bounds, beyond what is sufficient," while in action "thou stoppest short of what thou canst do."

The reason, he says, is not weak will: "So thou lovest not thyself, for if thou didst, thou wouldst love thy nature and her will." The craftsman in love with his art forgets to eat and wash; do you value your own nature "less than the turner values the turning art"? At the end it becomes clear what the work of a human being is: "the acts which concern society." One rises, then, for the common good.

**Doctrines.** Man is made not for things to happen to him but to act, and for him this is [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|life according to nature]]. And [[app-d-map-of-practice#^oikeiosis|men are born for one another]], so the work of a human being is a common work.

## 5.8

![[05-08#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

"Aesculapius prescribed to this man horse-exercise": Marcus reasons about fate by comparison with a physician. Aesculapius, the god of healing, "prescribes" to a patient riding, cold baths or going without shoes; it is unpleasant but good for health. In the same way the nature of the universe "prescribed to this man disease or mutilation or loss," with the one difference that this is prescribed not for health but as part of destiny.

A play on words follows, and Long signals it himself: "That which happens to (or, suits) every man." The Greek συμβαίνειν means both "to happen" and "to fit together": builders say that "squared stones in walls or the pyramids" are "suitable," "when they fit them to one another in some kind of connexion." So events "fit" us, and each lies in its place in the common masonry. "And as the universe is made up out of all bodies to be such a body as it is, so out of all existing causes necessity (destiny) is made up to be such a cause as it is."

It follows that what happens should be received as one receives a physician's prescriptions. "Many as a matter of course even among his prescriptions are disagreeable, but we accept them in the hope of health." This, however, leads not to your health but "to the health of the universe."

At the end Marcus names two reasons why one should be content with what happens. The first is that "it was done for thee and prescribed for thee," "originally from the most ancient causes spun with thy destiny"; the second, that it is needed by the whole. Discontent, then, is not merely useless: whoever complains cuts the connection of parts and causes "as far as it is in thy power." Long follows the Greek exactly: "the integrity of the whole is mutilated" (πηροῦται), "if thou cuttest off" (διακόπτεις), as one cuts a limb from a body.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^unity-of-cosmos|All things are bound into one whole]], and destiny is made up of all causes, as the world of all bodies. So what happens can not only be accepted, like a physician's prescription, but loved: in this lies [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|acceptance of fate]].

## 5.9

![[05-09#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

"Be not disgusted, nor discouraged, nor dissatisfied, if thou dost not succeed in doing everything according to right principles." Marcus is writing about failure. He does not demand perfection of himself; his measure is different: to return and "be content if the greater part of what thou doest is consistent with man's nature."

He tells himself to return to philosophy not "as if she were a master, but act like those who have sore eyes." Long's "master" stands for the Greek παιδαγωγός, the slave who took a child to school and watched over him. A child goes to such a minder reluctantly, from duty; people go to a remedy of their own accord, because it hurts: "a bit of sponge and egg" for sore eyes, "a plaster, or drenching with water." Philosophy here is not a minder but a treatment, and then, Marcus says, "thou wilt not fail to obey reason, and thou wilt repose in it."

He reminds himself that "philosophy requires only the things which thy nature requires," while he himself wants something else. Pleasure leads us astray precisely by seeming agreeable; but are not "magnanimity, freedom, simplicity, equanimity, piety" more agreeable? Most agreeable of all is understanding itself, when one thinks of "the security and the happy course" of what depends on it. The Greek is τὸ ἄπταιστον καὶ εὔρουν, "without stumbling and flowing well." We met the word εὔρουν in [[02-05|2.5]], where Zeno used it to define happiness; Long renders it differently in the two places, and the echo is lost.

**Doctrines.** Philosophy "requires only the things which thy nature requires," and so to return to it is to return to [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|life according to nature]].

## 5.16

![[05-16#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

"Such as are thy habitual thoughts, such also will be the character of thy mind; for the soul is dyed by the thoughts." The Greek is βάπτεται, "is dyed," like wool in dye, and Long keeps the image. We met it in [[03-04|3.4]], where the good man is "dyed deep with justice." Here we are told how this happens: the soul takes the color of what you think about most often. It follows that it must be dyed deliberately, by constantly repeating right thoughts: "Dye it then with a continuous series of such thoughts as these."

Marcus at once gives two examples of such thoughts. The first: "where a man can live, there he can also live well. But he must live in a palace; well then, he can also live well in a palace." The writer is an emperor who lives at court and who learned from Fronto ([[01-11|1.11]]) "what envy, and duplicity, and hypocrisy are in a tyrant." The court, then, cannot serve as an excuse.

The second example is a longer argument. Each thing is carried toward that for which it was made; in this is its end; "and where the end is, there also is the advantage and the good of each thing." "Now the good for the reasonable animal is society," in Greek κοινωνία. The inferior exist for the sake of the superior, "and the superior for one another."

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^oikeiosis|Men are born for one another]], and the good of a rational being lies in society.

## 5.23

![[05-23#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

The river again, as in [[04-43|4.43]], but now it is not only time that flows: "substance is like a river in a continual flow." The very substance of things flows, in Greek οὐσία; "the activities of things are in constant change, and the causes work in infinite varieties."

And beside us lies "this boundless abyss of the past and of the future in which all things disappear," and a man stands on the narrow strip of the present between two abysses.

Marcus puts the conclusion as a question: "How then is he not a fool who is puffed up with such things or plagued about them and makes himself miserable? for they vex him only for a time, and a short time." All three feelings, pride, agitation and complaint, assume that the thing will last long, and the stream removes them at once.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^unity-of-cosmos|All things are bound into one whole and flow without ceasing]]. One who often thinks how everything rushes past has no reason to be proud of what passes or to complain of it: all one can do is [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|accept]] it.

## 5.27

![[05-27#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

"Live with the gods." These words sound like a call to mysticism or to ritual. Marcus knew ritual well: as emperor he held, like every emperor since Augustus, the office of *pontifex maximus*, head of the Roman state religion. But the living with the gods he writes of here requires neither rites nor initiations; it is much simpler, and he explains at once what it consists of.

To live with the gods, in Greek συζῆν θεοῖς, is to show them one's soul constantly in two states. First, the soul must be "satisfied with that which is assigned to him": to complain of one's lot is to reproach the gods with having ordered the world badly. Second, the soul "does all that the daemon wishes."

We know the daemon (δαίμων) from Book III, but here Marcus speaks of it most clearly. Zeus has given it "to every man for his guardian and guide," and it is "a portion of himself," in Greek ἀπόσπασμα, literally "a piece broken off." Most important: "this is every man's understanding and reason." The inner god, then, is a man's own reason.

Zeus for the Stoics is not a bearded Olympian with thunderbolts but the reason that governs the world. If every man has a portion of this reason, then to obey one's own reason is to live with God, and there is no need to go to Olympus or to a temple to find him.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^self-is-hegemonikon|A man's true self is his reason]], a portion of the divine reason. [[app-d-map-of-practice#^providence|The world is ordered by providence]]. And the soul satisfied with its lot lives in [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|acceptance of fate]].

## 5.29

![[05-29#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

One can leave life as one leaves a room full of smoke: this short passage on freedom uses the Stoic image of the open door.

"As thou intendest to live when thou art gone out, so it is in thy power to live here." One can live truly, according to one's nature, now, putting nothing off.

Then Marcus speaks of the extreme case: if men do not permit you to live so, "then get away out of life, yet so as if thou wert suffering no harm." The Stoics allowed a reasoned departure from life when a worthy life becomes impossible, and we met this thought in [[03-01|3.1]]. The image of smoke in a room comes from Epictetus; in Marcus it is compressed into three Greek words, καπνὸς καὶ ἀπέρχομαι, which Long renders "The house is smoky, and I quit it." And he strips the act of heroism and pathos: "Why dost thou think that this is any trouble?"

The heart of the passage, however, is not the departure but what remains while the door is not needed: "so long as nothing of the kind drives me out, I remain, am free, and no man shall hinder me from doing what I choose." Freedom, then, lies not in circumstances but in the fact that no one can force me to will what is bad. And these choices are not arbitrary: "I choose to do what is according to the nature of the rational and social animal."

**Doctrines.** Circumstances are not in our power, but [[app-d-map-of-practice#^dichotomy-of-control|our choice is]], and the freedom to choose according to nature cannot be taken away. And to choose according to nature is to [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|live according to the nature]] of a rational and social being.

## The Book in Brief

**Key thought.** Book V begins in the morning with the question of what one rises for, and the whole of it answers that question ([[05-01|5.1]]). The work of a human being is a common work, because the good of a rational being lies in society ([[05-16|5.16]]). It also lies in acceptance of fate and in loyalty to one's own reason, the daemon that Zeus has given to every man as a portion of himself ([[05-27|5.27]]).

**Practice: the morning meditation.** Marcus's own formula is this: "I am rising to the work of a human being" ([[05-01|5.1]]); he tells himself to say it at once, when he does not want to get up. The exercise is, after waking and before business and people, to remind oneself of three things: what the day begins for; what may be difficult in it and how to answer it; and what everything rests on (the good lies only in virtue, externals do no harm, men are kin to one another). A few minutes are enough; one can also write it down: the *Meditations*, it seems, were partly such notes.
