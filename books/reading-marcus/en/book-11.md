## The Thread of the Book

**What the Rational Soul Can Do.** Book XI opens with the properties of the rational soul (11.1) and tests them on hard cases: on indifferent things (11.16), on anger (11.18), on the death of a child (11.33, 11.34). At the end, in the extracts from Epictetus, we find what seems to be the source of the three disciplines Marcus Aurelius practiced (11.37).

Eight key passages: the properties of the rational soul (11.1) → tragedy as a reminder (11.6) → wiping out what is written (11.16) → ten gifts against anger (11.18) → a morning look at the stars (11.27) → figs in winter (11.33) → ears of corn are reaped (11.34) → the three disciplines in Epictetus (11.37).

## Introduction

Book XI begins with an inventory of the properties of the rational soul (11.1). Its most vivid image presents judgment as writing on wax: one need not write it, and if it has been written, one can wipe it out at once (11.16); its most detailed passage is a set of ten "gifts" against anger (11.18).

The book also gives a direct answer to the bitter admission of Book VIII, that the life of a philosopher is no longer within Marcus's reach (8.1): "How plain does it appear that there is not another condition of life so well suited for philosophizing as this in which thou now happenest to be" (11.7).

At the end comes another section of extracts, as in Book VII: the fable of the mice, Socrates, Homer, Hesiod, and above all Epictetus (11.22–11.39).

The passages fall into several themes.

- **The properties of the rational soul:** 11.1, 11.12, 11.15, 11.16, 11.19, 11.20.
- **People and anger:** 11.8, 11.9, 11.13, 11.14, 11.18.
- **The common work and one's own art:** 11.4, 11.5, 11.7, 11.21.
- **The theater and the exercises of other schools:** 11.6, 11.26, 11.27.
- **Death and loss:** 11.3, 11.33, 11.34, 11.35.
- **Extracts from Socrates, Homer and Epictetus:** 11.22–11.25, 11.28–11.39.

Among the passages without commentary, note 11.12, with one of the finest images in the book: "The spherical form of the soul maintains its figure when it is neither extended towards any object, nor contracted inwards, nor dispersed, nor sinks down, but is illuminated by light." In antiquity the sphere was held to be the perfect form.

## 11.1

![[11-01#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

The first words of the book: "These are the properties of the rational soul." Marcus describes not what the soul ought to be, but what it already possesses.

The first three properties rise one above another: "it sees itself, analyses itself, and makes itself such as it chooses." First the soul sees itself, then takes itself apart, then shapes itself, and the object each time is the same: itself. The soul is its own craftsman and its own material. Next we read that "the fruit which it bears itself enjoys": the fruits of plants go to others, but the fruit of a good deed goes to the one who did it.

The main argument rests on a comparison with the stage. In a dance or a play, if anything cuts it short, "the whole action is incomplete." Not so here: "in every part and wherever it may be stopped, it makes what has been set before it full and complete, so that it can say, I have what is my own." The Greek is ἐγὼ ἀπέχω τὰ ἐμά. In business language the verb ἀπέχω meant "I have received in full," and it was written on receipts. A life cut short at any moment has still been paid in full. This is the same thought as in [[06-02|6.2]]: dying is one of the acts of life, and for it, too, it is enough to do well what is at hand.

Then follows the view from above, carried to its limit: the soul traverses "the whole universe, and the surrounding vacuum." According to the Stoics the world is one and bounded, wholly filled within, and around it stretches the void; so taught Zeno and Chrysippus, and Posidonius disputed only whether the void is infinite. The rational soul grasps in thought both the world and what lies beyond it, and "extends itself into the infinity of time." Hence the conclusion: "he who is forty years old, if he has any understanding at all," has already seen all that has been and all that will be.

The end of the passage turns to people: the rational soul has "love of one's neighbour, and truth and modesty." And the last sentence: "Thus the right reason differs not at all from the reason of justice." Justice is not something added to reason; it is reason itself turned toward others.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^virtue-is-sufficient|Virtue is sufficient for happiness]]: the fruit of a good deed goes to the doer, and in full at any moment. [[app-d-map-of-practice#^eternal-recurrence|Everything comes around again]], and a man of forty has seen it all.

## 11.6

![[11-06#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

Tragedy, the old comedy, the middle and the new pass before Marcus one after another, and of each genre he asks one thing: what use is it to the spectator?

Of tragedy he says that it was brought on the stage "as means of reminding men of the things which happen to them, and that it is according to nature for things to happen so." The Greek ὑπομνηστικαί, "reminding," comes from the same root as ὑπομνήματα: the theater here is not an art but an exercise of memory. The main argument is this: "if you are delighted with what is shown on the stage, you should not be troubled with that which takes place on the larger stage." In the theater we watch another's grief with pleasure; in life the same event seems unbearable; it follows that the event is not the cause. Marcus adds that such things are borne even by those "who cry out 'O Cithaeron.'" This is the cry of Oedipus in Sophocles when he learns the truth about himself, and even he bears his fate.

Then come three extracts from Euripides. Marcus has already copied them separately in Book VII, in the section of extracts ([[07-38|7.38]], [[07-40|7.40]], [[07-41|7.41]]); here he gathers them together and drops the second line of two of them.

The old comedy, he says, was useful by its very plainness of speaking "in reminding men to beware of insolence," and even Diogenes took from it. Of the middle and new comedy, which "gradually sank down into a mere mimic artifice," Marcus passes no verdict but asks a question: "the whole plan of such poetry and dramaturgy, to what end does it look?" It is the same question that in [[07-04|7.4]] he told himself to put to every act: to what end?

**Doctrines.** Tragedy reminds us that things happen according to nature, and so they are to be [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|accepted]] on the larger stage as on the smaller. This is to [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|live according to nature]].

## 11.16

![[11-16#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

"As to living in the best way, this power is in the soul, if it be indifferent to things which are indifferent." The word appears twice. "Things which are indifferent" is a technical term of the Stoic school for everything that is neither good nor evil, such as health, wealth, fame and death; "be indifferent" speaks of a person's attitude to these things. The things are indifferent by nature, but to be indifferent to them must still be learned.

How is this to be reached? By looking at each of these things "separately and all together," as Long rightly renders it, and by remembering "that not one of them produces in us an opinion about itself, nor comes to us; but these things remain immovable, and it is we ourselves who produce the judgments about them."

Here too is the clearest image in the book: we produce these judgments and, "as we may say, write them in ourselves, it being in our power not to write them, and it being in our power, if perchance these judgments have imperceptibly got admission to our minds, to wipe them out." The soul is like a wax tablet. A judgment is not an event in the world but writing made by our own hand, and so it can be wiped out. The qualification matters: Marcus allows that the writing may appear without our will, and he asks not for infallibility but for quickness in wiping it out. Long's "wipe out" renders the same verb we saw in [[07-29|7.29]] and [[08-47|8.47]].

There is comfort here too: "such attention will only be for a short time, and then life will be at an end." Usually the brevity of life cheapens what we desire; here it lightens the work itself: the watch will not be long.

The passage ends thus: if something is contrary to nature, "seek what is conformable to thy own nature, and strive towards this, even if it bring no reputation; for every man is allowed to seek his own good."

**Doctrines.** Things remain immovable, and it is we who write our judgments about them, so those judgments are [[app-d-map-of-practice#^dichotomy-of-control|in our power]]. And to seek one's own good, even if it brings no reputation, is to [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|live according to one's nature]].

## 11.18

![[11-18#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

In [[09-42|9.42]] there were five remedies against anger; here there are nine, and a tenth besides: it is the fullest set in the *Meditations*. Marcus calls them gifts of the Muses, as though he had received them from above rather than gathered them himself from the Stoic tradition.

The first six help one see the offender and the offense differently. Men are made for one another, and you were set over them "as a ram over the flock or a bull over the herd": he compares himself not to a shepherd but to a leader of his own kind. Consider what they are "at table, in bed, and so forth," and under what compulsion of their opinions they act. If they do wrong, "they do so involuntarily and in ignorance." You yourself are no better, and if you abstain from some fault, it may be "through cowardice, or concern about reputation." "Thou dost not even understand whether men are doing wrong or not," he tells himself, since much is done with reference to circumstances. And life is too short to spend on vexation: "after a short time we are all laid out dead."

The seventh remedy is the core of the set, and we know it already: "it is not men's acts which disturb us... but it is our own opinions which disturb us. Take away these opinions then... and thy anger is gone." The Greek has the same verb as [[04-07|4.7]], ἆρον, "take away," and Long renders it the same way in both places. Another's act lives in another's soul; it enters yours only through your judgment of it.

The eighth remedy: the consequences of anger are more painful than what caused it. The ninth: "a good disposition is invincible if it be genuine." To the man who is trying to do you harm at this very moment one should say calmly: "Not so, my child: we are constituted by nature for something else: I shall certainly not be injured, but thou art injuring thyself, my child." And say it "neither with any double meaning nor in the way of reproach," "not as if thou wert lecturing him, nor yet that any bystander may admire."

Then Marcus overturns the usual notion of strength: "to be moved by passion is not manly, but that mildness and gentleness, as they are more agreeable to human nature, so also are they more manly." "For in the same degree in which a man's mind is nearer to freedom from all passion, in the same degree also is it nearer to strength." "For he who yields to pain and he who yields to anger, both are wounded and both submit."

The tenth gift comes from the leader of the Muses himself: "to expect bad men not to do wrong is madness." But to allow that they behave so to others and to expect them not to wrong you "is irrational and tyrannical." Each time we are angry, thinking "how could he do this to me," we demand for ourselves an exemption from human nature.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^oikeiosis|Men are born for one another]]. [[app-d-map-of-practice#^providence|The world is ordered by providence]]. And [[app-d-map-of-practice#^no-harm-to-virtuous|no harm can be done to the virtuous]]: "I shall certainly not be injured, but thou art injuring thyself."

**Commentary.**

The Pythagoreans bid us "in the morning look to the heavens," and Marcus takes this gesture from them. The time is chosen with care: in the morning the day's business has not yet seized the attention.

The stars are given as an example because each does its own work, and always "in the same manner." The morning passages [[05-01|5.1]] and [[08-12|8.12]] spoke of the same thing: to rise for one's own work. Then three words are named: order, purity, "nudity." Marcus explains the last himself: "For there is no veil over a star." A star hides nothing and covers itself with nothing, and what it does is visible to all. The Greek προκάλυμμα means both "veil" and "pretext, excuse." Marcus does not state the conclusion; perhaps it is this: to do one's work in the open, without covers and without excuses. A similar exercise came in [[07-47|7.47]], where Marcus proposed to share in thought the cosmic motion of the stars.

**Doctrines.** To do one's work without a veil, like the stars, is [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|life according to nature]].

## 11.33

**Commentary.**

The comparison of figs in winter with a dead child is taken from Epictetus, and Marcus copies it without a name: Epictetus will be named in the next passage.

The comparison rests not on insensibility but on the logic of the impossible: to look for figs in winter and to look for a child who is gone are both demands for what is not there. No one is angry with winter for having no figs, because everyone understands that the season has passed.

Long is exact here: "when it is no longer allowed," for the Greek ὅτε οὐκέτι δίδοται, literally "when it is no longer given." The child was given, and the gift has ended; for such a loss there is no one to blame.

Marcus does not say that grief is forbidden. What is called mad is to "look for" the child (ζητεῖν), that is, to demand back what is gone. What is condemned, then, is not the feeling but the demand for the impossible.

It is worth remembering who copies this out. Many of Marcus's children died in infancy, and he did not copy these words out as theory.

**Doctrines.** [[app-d-map-of-practice#^externals-on-loan|Nothing is our own]]: even children are given for a time. And to accept what is no longer given, without demanding the impossible, is to come to [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|acceptance of fate]].

## 11.34

![[11-34#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

When a man kisses his child, Epictetus advises, he should say to himself: "To-morrow perchance thou wilt die." Then comes the objection of an interlocutor and the answer that this word "expresses any work of nature."

The words are said inwardly, during the caress; the Greek ἔνδον means "within," and Long's "whisper to himself" conveys it. Tenderness is not cancelled, and the child knows nothing of it. And the formula is careful: "perchance," a possibility, not a prophecy. This is the anticipation of evils familiar from [[02-01|2.1]], carried to the most painful place.

The objection is natural. "Words of bad omen" are in Greek δύσφημον, an ill word, one not to be spoken lest it bring misfortune. Epictetus does not argue with the fear but redefines the word. His thought seems to be this: an ill word is one by which people fear to call down misfortune, while a word that simply names the course of nature calls nothing down. The ear of corn has already appeared in [[11-06|11.6]], in the line from Euripides: "Life's harvest reap like the wheat's fruitful ear."

**Doctrines.** Death is one of the works of nature, and readiness to accept it beforehand belongs to [[app-d-map-of-practice#^amor-fati|acceptance of fate]]. And to call the works of nature by their names, without fear, is to [[app-d-map-of-practice#^live-according-to-nature|live according to nature]].

**Commentary.**

This extract from Epictetus contains the very three disciplines that Marcus named in his own words in [[08-07|8.7]] and [[09-06|9.6]]: assent, action, desire. Their source is Epictetus, who in the *Discourses* explicitly names three fields of exercise (III.2); it was Pierre Hadot, as noted at [[08-07|8.7]], who called them disciplines.

Of assent the Greek says not "lay down rules" but "discover an art," τέχνη, and Long keeps it: assent must be learned as a craft is learned. For the movements of the soul three conditions are given. First, that they be made, in Long's words, "with regard to circumstances"; the Greek μεθ' ὑπεξαιρέσεως is more precise, "with reservation," that is, to strive while remembering that the outcome is not in our power: "if nothing prevents." Second, that they "be consistent with social interests." Third, that they "have regard to the value of the object." Of desire it is said that one should keep away from it altogether; Long's "sensual desire" narrows the Greek ὄρεξις, which means desire of every kind. And one should show avoidance toward none of "the things which are not in our power."

**Doctrines.** All three disciplines, of assent, of action and of desire, are built on one distinction: [[app-d-map-of-practice#^dichotomy-of-control|what is in our power and what is not]].

## The Book in Brief

**Key thought.** Everything needed for a good life the rational soul already has: it shapes itself, and its work is complete at any moment, wherever it is cut short ([[11-01|11.1]]). What torments us is not the thing but the judgment about it, and the soul can wipe that judgment out ([[11-16|11.16]]). So even offense and loss do not take its work from it ([[11-18|11.18]], [[11-33|11.33]]).

**Practice: keeping at hand (πρόχειρον).** Marcus ends his set against anger with the words: "Remember these nine rules, as if thou hadst received them as a gift from the Muses" ([[11-18|11.18]]). The exercise is to reduce the teaching to short formulas and keep them ready, so that the formula comes at the moment the impression arrives, not after. The gifts against anger are such a store: "a good disposition is invincible if it be genuine," "to be moved by passion is not manly." In a fit of anger it is enough that even one of them comes to mind.
