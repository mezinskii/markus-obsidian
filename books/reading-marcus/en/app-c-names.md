The first book of the *Meditations* consists almost entirely of names. Grandfather, father, mother, great-grandfather, teachers, adoptive father: to each Marcus Aurelius is grateful for something specific, and these short passages show the people who formed him. Later, names appear less often and serve another purpose. Hadrian and Augustus are reminders that even the greatest power is soon forgotten; Alexander the Great is made equal in death with his muleteer. Heraclitus, Socrates, Plato, Chrysippus and Epictetus come as interlocutors whose words Marcus copies out for himself.

Below are the people about whom Marcus says more than a name in a list. Minor names from lists (Celer, Panthea, Charax and others) are not included. On Thrasea, Helvidius, Cato, Dion and Brutus, see the commentary on [[01-14|1.14]]. Places are given by the passages where a person is named or clearly meant; where there are many, the chief ones are given. Each person is listed under the name by which he or she is known in the book, and the full name is given at the start of the entry. Three people are called Verus in the *Meditations*: Marcus's grandfather ([[01-01|1.1]]), his father ([[08-25|8.25]]) and his adoptive brother Lucius Verus ([[01-17|1.17]], [[08-37|8.37]]); each has his own entry. At the end of the index are gathered those whom Marcus never names but who belonged to his circle: without them some places in the book are not fully understood.

## Family and the Imperial House

### Hadrian

76–138; emperor from 117. In 138 the childless Hadrian adopted Antoninus Pius on condition that he in turn adopt the young Marcus and Lucius Verus: so Hadrian opened Marcus's way to power. In the *Meditations* he appears not as a relative but as an example: if even a recent all-powerful ruler is already forgotten, concern for posthumous fame is empty. In 4.33 and 8.5 he is named together with Augustus. Mentioned: [[04-33|4.33]], [[08-05|8.5]], [[08-25|8.25]], [[08-37|8.37]], [[10-27|10.27]].

### Antoninus Pius

86–161; emperor 138–161. Marcus's adoptive father and predecessor. To him is devoted 1.16, the longest passage of Book I: a detailed portrait of the ruler Marcus wished to resemble. A second portrait is given in 6.30, and in 4.33, 8.25 and 10.27 Antoninus stands in the row of the dead together with Hadrian. In 9.21, where Marcus recalls his life "under thy grandfather, then... under thy mother, then... under thy father," the father is probably Antoninus too. Mentioned: [[01-16|1.16]], [[01-17|1.17]], [[04-33|4.33]], [[06-30|6.30]], [[08-25|8.25]], [[09-21|9.21]], [[10-27|10.27]].

### Domitia Lucilla

c. 100 – between 155 and 161. Marcus's mother, heiress to a great fortune, a patron of Greek philosophers and rhetoricians. In 1.3 Marcus thanks her for piety, generosity and a simple way of life. 1.7 mentions a letter that Rusticus wrote to her from Sinuessa, and in 8.25 she is apparently the Lucilla who "saw Verus die," that is, her husband, Marcus's father. In 1.17 Marcus writes that it was "my mother's fate to die young," though by these dates she was over fifty; how to reconcile this is unclear: perhaps the dates are inexact. Mentioned: [[01-03|1.3]], [[01-07|1.7]], [[01-17|1.17]], [[08-25|8.25]], [[09-21|9.21]].

### Catilius Severus

Lucius Catilius Severus, c. 60/70 – c. 138/139. Senator, consul in 110 and 120, prefect of Rome under Hadrian; in 138 he lost the prefecture: according to the *Historia Augusta*, he had hoped to become Hadrian's heir himself. He was Marcus's step-great-grandfather on his mother's side: the husband of Dasumia Polla, widow of Cn. Domitius Tullus, who had adopted Marcus's grandmother. They were not related by blood, but as a child Marcus also bore his name: Marcus Annius Catilius Severus. In 1.4 Marcus thanks his great-grandfather that he was taught at home by good teachers, not in the public schools. Mentioned: [[01-04|1.4]].

### Lucius Verus

130–169. Marcus's adoptive brother: in 138 Antoninus Pius, on Hadrian's condition, adopted them together. From 161 Marcus's co-emperor and husband of his daughter Lucilla; he died in 169, perhaps of the plague. In 1.17 Marcus thanks the gods for a brother "who was able by his moral character to rouse me to vigilance over myself" (it seems, by bad example) and who pleased him "by his respect and affection"; in 8.37 his tomb is recalled. Mentioned: [[01-17|1.17]], [[08-37|8.37]].

### Marcus Annius Verus

c. 50 – 138. Marcus's paternal grandfather, three times consul (97, 121 and 126) and prefect of Rome; he took Marcus into his house after the early death of his father. The passage about him opens the whole book: from his grandfather Marcus learned "good morals and the government of my temper." Mentioned: [[01-01|1.1]], [[01-17|1.17]], [[09-21|9.21]].

### Marcus Annius Verus the Younger

c. 90 – c. 124. Marcus's father, senator and praetor; he died young, when his son was about three. Marcus hardly remembered him, and in 1.2 he learns not from his father but "from the reputation and remembrance" of him: "modesty and a manly character." In 8.25 he is apparently the Verus whom his wife Lucilla saw die. Mentioned: [[01-02|1.2]], [[08-25|8.25]].

### Faustina the Younger

c. 130 – 175/176. Daughter of Antoninus Pius and Faustina the Elder, Marcus's cousin and from 145 his wife; mother of all his children, of whom there were more than ten, among them Commodus and Lucilla. She accompanied Marcus on campaign and received the title "Mother of the Camps." In 1.17 Marcus thanks the gods for such a wife. He says nothing more of her, but what he says is warm. Later stories of her profligacy are open to doubt. Mentioned: [[01-17|1.17]].

### Faustina the Elder

c. 100 – 140/141. Sister of Marcus's father, wife of Antoninus Pius and mother of Faustina the Younger. She died early in her husband's reign and was deified. In the *Meditations* she appears once, in a roll call of deaths: "Antoninus saw Faustina die, and then Antoninus died." Mentioned: [[08-25|8.25]].

## Teachers and Mentors

### Alexander the Grammarian

c. 80/90 – c. 150. Alexander of Cotiaeum, the foremost Greek grammarian and interpreter of Homer of his generation; he taught Marcus Greek language and literature. Aelius Aristides was also among his pupils. In 1.10 Marcus learns tact from him: not to reproach one who has made a slip in a word, but to use the right expression in one's own reply. Mentioned: [[01-10|1.10]].

### Alexander the Platonist

Second century. The only teacher of Book I whom Marcus explicitly calls a Platonist. He is traditionally identified with the sophist Alexander "Clay-Plato," who was Marcus's secretary for Greek correspondence, but the identification is disputed. In 1.12 Marcus learns from him not to plead lack of leisure without need, so as not to neglect under this pretext his duties to those with whom he lives. Mentioned: [[01-12|1.12]].

### Apollonius of Chalcedon

First half of the second century. A Stoic summoned to Rome by Antoninus Pius to teach the heir. In 1.8 Marcus presents him as a living example: resolution together with gentleness, constancy of reason in pain, the loss of a child and illness. In 1.17 Marcus thanks the gods that he knew him, Rusticus and Maximus. Mentioned: [[01-08|1.8]], [[01-17|1.17]].

### Bacchius

First half of the second century. The first of the philosophers whom Marcus heard as a boy on Diognetus's advice. Perhaps he is the Platonist Bacchius known from Galen. Mentioned: [[01-06|1.6]].

### Diognetus

First to second century; he probably taught Marcus in the 130s. According to the biography of Marcus, a teacher of painting, and according to 1.6 the one who led the boy to philosophy, apparently around 132–133, when Marcus was eleven or twelve. Marcus owes to him his disbelief in miracle-workers, his tolerance of free speech and his first attempts: boyhood dialogues, a wish for "a plank bed and skin." Mentioned: [[01-06|1.6]].

### Claudius Maximus

Second century; suffect consul around 142 or 144. A Stoic and senator, later proconsul of Africa: around 158/159 he presided over the trial of Apuleius, and this is the only detailed testimony to his character independent of Marcus. In 1.15 Marcus gives one of the fullest portraits in the book: self-command, cheerfulness in illness, evenness, sincerity. His illness is recalled at the end of 1.16, and in 8.25 we read that "Secunda saw Maximus die"; Secunda was apparently his wife. Mentioned: [[01-15|1.15]], [[01-16|1.16]], [[01-17|1.17]], [[08-25|8.25]].

### Claudius Severus

Gnaeus Claudius Severus Arabianus, born c. 113; apparently still alive in 176. A senator from Pompeiopolis, a Peripatetic philosopher, consul in 146; the only Peripatetic among the teachers of Book I. His son, also Gnaeus Claudius Severus, married a daughter of Marcus, so the teacher became Marcus's relative by marriage. He is usually taken to be the Severus whom Marcus in 1.14 calls "my brother" and thanks for introducing him to Thrasea, Helvidius, Cato, Dion and Brutus; why Marcus calls him "brother" is unclear, perhaps because of this tie or simply from closeness. In the seventeenth century Gataker proposed reading "friend." Mentioned: [[01-14|1.14]].

### Marcianus

First half of the second century. The third of the philosophers whom Marcus heard as a boy, after Bacchius and Tandasis. Beyond this mention almost nothing is known of him. Mentioned: [[01-06|1.6]].

### Rusticus

Quintus Junius Rusticus, c. 100 – after 170. A Stoic, apparently Marcus's chief philosophical mentor; probably the grandson of Arulenus Rusticus, who was executed under Domitian. Suffect consul in 133 and ordinary consul in 162, then prefect of Rome; Marcus consulted him even after coming to power, and as prefect Rusticus presided over the trial of Justin Martyr. In 1.7 Marcus thanks him for his aversion to sophistry and display, and for lending him from his own library the records of Epictetus's discourses; in 1.17 he admits that he was "often out of humour with Rusticus," but never did anything he had occasion to repent. Mentioned: [[01-07|1.7]], [[01-17|1.17]].

### Sextus of Chaeroneia

Second century. A philosopher, a relative of Plutarch; the *Historia Augusta* calls him Plutarch's *nepos*, nephew or grandson. He taught at Athens and Rome and lived to old age, so that Marcus attended his lectures even as emperor. In 1.9 Marcus learns from him "the idea of living conformably to nature," to tolerate the ignorant and to join affection for people with freedom from passion, and thanks him for "the example of a family governed in a fatherly manner." Mentioned: [[01-09|1.9]].

### Tandasis

First half of the second century. The second of the philosophers whom Marcus heard as a boy on Diognetus's advice; known only from this mention. Mentioned: [[01-06|1.6]].

### Fronto

Marcus Cornelius Fronto, c. 95 – c. 167. The leading Latin orator of the age, suffect consul around 142–143; from the late 130s and through the 140s he taught Marcus Latin eloquence. Their surviving correspondence remains the main contemporary source for Marcus's youth. In 1.11 Marcus thanks him not for rhetoric but for understanding what tyranny is and how deficient in natural affection those called patricians can be. Mentioned: [[01-11|1.11]].

### Cinna Catulus

Second century; he probably taught Marcus in the late 130s and the 140s. A Stoic named in the list of Marcus's teachers in his biography; almost everything we know of him is said in 1.13. There Marcus learns from him not to be indifferent when a friend finds fault, even without reason, to speak well of teachers and to love his children truly. Mentioned: [[01-13|1.13]].

## Philosophers

### Heraclitus

c. 540 – c. 480 BCE. The Ionian natural philosopher called "the Obscure," one of the chief Presocratic sources of Stoic physics. In 4.46 Marcus copies out several of his sayings, and in 6.42 recalls his words about sleepers, who also co-operate in what happens in the world. In 3.3 he notes that the man who taught the world conflagration died of dropsy, and in 8.3 he sets him beside Diogenes and Socrates against the conquerors. Mentioned: [[03-03|3.3]], [[04-46|4.46]], [[06-42|6.42]], [[06-47|6.47]], [[08-03|8.3]].

### Democritus

c. 460 – c. 370 BCE. With Leucippus, the founder of the atomic theory. For Marcus he stands for the rival picture of the world: in the alternative "providence or atoms" the atoms go back to him, though Marcus usually does not name him. In 4.24 Marcus corrects his rule about doing few things: one should do what is necessary; in 3.3 he recalls his death. In 7.31 Long writes "The poet says," but the words are Democritus's. Mentioned: [[03-03|3.3]], [[04-24|4.24]], [[07-31|7.31]].

### Diogenes

c. 412/404 – 323 BCE. The founder of Cynicism as a way of life, nicknamed "the Dog"; through Crates and Zeno he stands at the source of the Stoic tradition. In 8.3 he, with Heraclitus and Socrates, is set against Alexander, Gaius Caesar and Pompey. In 11.6 Marcus notes that Diogenes took something from the old comedy with its free speech. Mentioned: [[08-03|8.3]], [[11-06|11.6]].

### Monimus

Second half of the fourth century BCE. A Cynic, pupil of Diogenes and Crates. In 2.15 Marcus cites him in support of his own thought: "all is opinion." And he adds at once that one should take from the Cynic's words only what is true in them, that is, perhaps, not take them literally. Mentioned: [[02-15|2.15]].

### Plato

428/427–348/347 BCE. Founder of the Academy, pupil of Socrates. Marcus takes from him above all passages on death and on looking at human life from a height: Book VII contains a whole set of extracts from the *Republic*, the *Apology* and the *Gorgias*. In 9.29 Marcus warns himself not to expect Plato's Republic but to be content if the smallest thing goes on well. Mentioned: [[07-35|7.35]], [[07-44|7.44]], [[07-45|7.45]], [[07-46|7.46]], [[07-48|7.48]], [[07-63|7.63]], [[09-29|9.29]], [[10-23|10.23]].

### Socrates

c. 470 – 399 BCE. For the Stoics the model of the sage, and Marcus returns to him, perhaps, more often than to any other philosopher. He recalls his death, his answer to Perdiccas, his habit of calling the opinions of the many bugbears to frighten children. In 7.66 Marcus notes that Socrates's famous death and deeds prove nothing by themselves: one must look at what kind of soul he had. Mentioned: [[01-16|1.16]], [[03-03|3.3]], [[03-06|3.6]], [[06-47|6.47]], [[07-19|7.19]], [[07-66|7.66]], [[08-03|8.3]], [[11-23|11.23]], [[11-25|11.25]] and elsewhere.

### Theophrastus

c. 371 – 287 BCE. Aristotle's successor at the head of the Lyceum. In 2.10 Marcus approvingly cites his judgment that "the offences which are committed through desire are more blameable than those which are committed through anger," because the angry man turns away from reason "with a certain pain," while the one who yields to pleasure shows intemperance. Apart from him, Book II names only the Cynic Monimus (2.15). Mentioned: [[02-10|2.10]].

### Chrysippus

c. 279 – c. 206 BCE. The third head of the Stoa, who brought its logic, physics and ethics into a system. In 6.42 Marcus recalls his comparison: even a ridiculous verse is needed by the comedy as a whole, and so the fault-finder has his place in the world. But Marcus does not want to be such a verse. In 7.19 "many a Chrysippus" stands among those whom time has already swallowed. Mentioned: [[06-42|6.42]], [[07-19|7.19]].

### Epictetus

c. 50 – c. 135. A Stoic, pupil of Musonius Rufus; he wrote nothing himself, and his *Discourses* and *Handbook* were recorded by his pupil Arrian. He is the chief direct source of Marcus's Stoicism: from Epictetus come the three fields of exercise (Hadot calls them the three disciplines) and the distinction between what is in our power and what is not. Marcus received the records of his discourses from Rusticus (1.7), quotes him in 4.41, and at the end of Book XI gathers a set of his sayings. Mentioned: [[01-07|1.7]], [[04-41|4.41]], [[07-19|7.19]], [[11-34|11.34]], [[11-36|11.36]], [[11-37|11.37]], [[11-38|11.38]].

### Epicurus

341–270 BCE. Founder of the chief rival school. Marcus names him twice: in 7.64 he cites his thought that pain is "neither intolerable nor everlasting," and in 9.41 he holds him up as an example of one who went on with philosophy even in sickness. The atoms in the alternative "providence or atoms" are also Epicurean, though his name does not appear there. Mentioned: [[07-64|7.64]], [[09-41|9.41]].

## Rulers of the Past

### Augustus

63 BCE – 14 CE. The first Roman emperor. For Marcus he is the utmost measure of power and fame, which time nonetheless swallows; he is usually named together with Hadrian. In 8.31 Marcus lists the wife, daughter, friends and counselors of Augustus and concludes: "the whole court is dead." Mentioned: [[04-33|4.33]], [[08-05|8.5]], [[08-31|8.31]].

### Alexander the Great

356–323 BCE. King of Macedon, pupil of Aristotle, conqueror of the Persian empire. For Marcus he is the model of the futility of power: in 6.24 death makes him equal with his muleteer, and in 8.3 he, with Gaius Caesar and Pompey, gives place to Diogenes, Heraclitus and Socrates. Mentioned: [[03-03|3.3]], [[06-24|6.24]], [[08-03|8.3]], [[09-29|9.29]], [[10-27|10.27]].

## Not Named in the *Meditations*

### Plutarch of Chaeroneia

c. 45 – c. 125. A Platonist, author of the *Parallel Lives* and the *Moralia*. He was not Marcus's teacher, but a relative of his teacher Sextus, and through Sextus his influence may be felt in Book I. Cato, Dion and Brutus of 1.14 were probably known to Marcus from Plutarch's *Lives* as well. See: [[01-09|1.9]], [[01-14|1.14]].

### Aelius Aristides

c. 117 – c. 181. The best-known orator of the Second Sophistic under the Antonines. He was not Marcus's teacher but was connected with his circle: he studied with Alexander the Grammarian and wrote a funeral oration for him. In 176 Marcus heard him at Smyrna, and after an earthquake destroyed the city, Aristides asked Marcus for help by letter, and Smyrna was rebuilt. His *Roman Oration* praises the empire under Antoninus Pius, and his *Sacred Tales*, with their cures received in dreams, may be compared with the line of 1.17 about remedies shown to Marcus in dreams. See: [[01-10|1.10]], [[01-16|1.16]], [[01-17|1.17]].
