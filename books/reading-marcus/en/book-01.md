## The Thread of the Book

**The People of Marcus Aurelius.** Seventeen passages on qualities received from other people: from them Marcus Aurelius composes a portrait of himself, and at the end he thanks the gods for everything.

The book moves through four points: philosophy as treatment (1.7) → learning to recognize tyranny (1.11) → the ideal of rule (1.14) → the man who embodied that ideal (1.16).

## Introduction

The first book of the *Meditations* is unlike the other eleven. It says not a word about Marcus's own death or about the nature of the whole. All seventeen passages follow one formula: a person, and the quality Marcus received from that person.

"From my grandfather Verus I learned good morals and the government of my temper," Long translates. But there is no "I learned" in the Greek. Marcus writes: Παρὰ τοῦ πάππου Οὐήρου τὸ καλόηθες καὶ ἀόργητον, "from my grandfather Verus, goodness of character and freedom from anger." A preposition, a name, a quality: no verb and no words of thanks. The gratitude is only implied.

Book I, then, is best understood as an exercise with a simple rule: a person, and what came from that person. Its point seems to be to see clearly what one has received from the people around one: relatives, friends, teachers and mentors. Anyone can make such a list.

In the other books Marcus repeats his doctrines again and again; in the first there are none. Here his exercise works not with formulas but with people, recalling who gave him what. In a hard moment it is easier to remember how Antoninus bore himself than an abstract rule, and Marcus himself advises in [[06-48|6.48]]: "think of the virtues of those who live with thee... Wherefore we must keep them before us." That is why the commentaries on this book are arranged differently: they are about people, not doctrines.

The order of the passages has its own logic. First comes the family, what is given by birth (1.1–1.4); then the teachers Marcus chose for himself (1.5–1.15). Apart from these, and several times longer than any other passage, he speaks of his adoptive father, the emperor Antoninus Pius (1.16). The last passage is addressed to the gods (1.17).

Some scholars think that this book was written separately from the rest, and perhaps later. This cannot be proved, but its completeness speaks in favor of such a reading: unlike the others, it has a clear design and an ending.

There are five key passages in this book: [[01-07|1.7]] (Rusticus and philosophy as treatment), [[01-11|1.11]] (Fronto, close friend and teacher), [[01-14|1.14]] (Severus and the ideal of rule), [[01-16|1.16]] (Antoninus, the embodiment of that ideal) and [[01-17|1.17]] (the gods). The people of the other passages are described in the [[app-c-names|index of names]] and at [readaurelius.org/people](https://readaurelius.org/people).

## 1.7

![[01-07#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

Quintus Junius Rusticus was, by all accounts, the most important of Marcus's teachers, and he receives one of the longest passages in the book.

Marcus had tutors and teachers before Rusticus, but only after meeting him did philosophy become his life's work. The list of lessons is long, yet three layers of meaning can be made out in it.

The first layer is the correction of character. Marcus thanks Rusticus not for instruction but because from him "I received the impression that my character required improvement and discipline." The Greek is stronger: λαβεῖν φαντασίαν τοῦ χρῄζειν διορθώσεως καὶ θεραπείας τοῦ ἤθους, "to receive the impression that my character needed correction and **treatment**." Long's "discipline" loses the medical word. Θεραπεία is the very treatment spoken of in the introduction: philosophy as a remedy to which one returns again and again.

The second layer is what Rusticus helped him give up: sophistic emulation, writing on speculative matters, "delivering little hortatory orations," making a display of himself as an ascetic or a benefactor, and "rhetoric, and poetry, and fine writing." The verb deserves attention: ἀποστῆναι ῥητορικῆς means "to stand away from rhetoric," to withdraw from it, and Long's "abstain from" comes close. One can only withdraw from something one has been doing, and the young Marcus had indeed studied rhetoric with Fronto ([[01-11|1.11]]).

The third layer concerns Epictetus. The last line reads: "I am indebted to him for being acquainted with the discourses of Epictetus, which he communicated to me out of his own collection." What Long calls "discourses" are in the Greek ὑπομνήματα, the same word Marcus uses for his own notes in [[03-14|3.14]]; Long renders it differently in the two places, and the connection disappears. These were, it seems, the notes of Epictetus's lectures made by his pupil Arrian. Rusticus lent them to Marcus, and they apparently made a strong impression: Epictetus would become one of the authors Marcus quotes most often in the *Meditations*.

Rusticus remained close afterward as well. As emperor, according to the *Historia Augusta*, Marcus shared with him all his counsels, public and private, and in [[01-17|1.17]] he thanks the gods that, "though I was often out of humour with Rusticus, I never did anything of which I had occasion to repent." Rusticus, it seems, was a strict teacher, and Marcus valued him precisely for his strictness.

**Who Junius Rusticus was.** A Roman senator and Stoic philosopher, ordinary consul in 162 (under Marcus and Lucius Verus), later prefect of Rome. He was probably the grandson of Arulenus Rusticus, a Stoic senator who opposed Domitian and was executed in 93 for a eulogy of Thrasea Paetus. Junius Rusticus was thus a direct heir of the **"Stoic opposition"** of the first century, the line that ran from Thrasea through Helvidius to Arulenus (more on them at [[01-14|1.14]]).

## 1.11

![[01-11#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

Fronto was the finest Latin orator of his time and Marcus's teacher of eloquence. Their correspondence survives; it can be read in C. R. Haines's translation at [readaurelius.org/fronto](https://readaurelius.org/fronto). The letters were discovered in 1815 in a palimpsest, a parchment that in the Middle Ages had been scraped and written over with another text. They are remarkably tender: teacher and pupil address each other with the warmest endearments.

Yet Marcus does not thank Fronto for anything he taught: not a word about eloquence, style or Latin. From years of study with Fronto he kept one thing: an understanding of the envy, changeability and pretense that go with tyranny, and the observation that those called patricians at Rome are "rather deficient in paternal affection."

The verb here is ἐπιστῆσαι, not "to teach" but "to make one stop and notice," and Long's "to observe" conveys it. What was to be noticed is named in three words. **βασκανία** means not simply envy but the evil eye, ill will that looks for a way to harm. **ποικιλία** means many-coloredness, the quality of a man who has no single face; Long's "duplicity" narrows it. **ὑπόκρισις** literally means an actor's performance, a mask. The evil eye, changeability and the mask: perhaps a description of the court at which the future emperor was to live.

The second half of the sentence is cautious (ὡς ἐπίπαν… πως, "generally... rather"), and Long keeps the caution. It also contains a play on words. Those "who are called Patricians" are in Marcus's Greek **εὐπατρίδαι**, literally "those with good fathers," and they are **ἀστοργότεροι**, from στοργή, natural family affection, the love of a parent for a child. Long's "deficient in paternal affection" catches the play well: the men whose dignity rests on their fathers are the very ones who do not know how to love as kin.

Marcus grew up and moved from rhetoric to philosophy, and Fronto took it hard: he argued with his pupil, tried to win him back to eloquence, and was jealous of philosophy. Just above, in [[01-07|1.7]], Marcus thanks Rusticus for teaching him "to abstain from rhetoric, and poetry," and in the last passage of the book he thanks the gods "that I did not make more proficiency in rhetoric, poetry, and the other studies." Fronto's craft did not take root in him, and Marcus counts this a piece of luck, though he loved his teacher.

A few passages later, in [[01-16|1.16]], Marcus speaks of Antoninus, who belonged to the same nobility but was an exception to it. And in Book VI ([[06-30|6.30]]) he warns himself: ὅρα μὴ ἀποκαισαρωθῇς, "Take care that thou art not made into a Caesar, that thou art not dyed with this dye," that is, do not become what power makes of a man.

## 1.14

**Commentary.**

Who "my brother Severus" was is unknown. Marcus had no brothers by birth; his adoptive brother was called Verus and did not deserve such praise. Most likely this is Claudius Severus, a Peripatetic philosopher, and "brother" is either a copyist's error or a mark of closeness. For the sense of the passage this hardly matters; what matters is what Marcus received from him.

What he received, unexpectedly for an emperor, was a list of opponents of one-man rule. Thrasea Paetus was a Stoic senator whom Nero forced to take his own life; Helvidius Priscus, his son-in-law, was exiled and executed under Vespasian; Cato the Younger, the republican, stabbed himself at Utica rather than accept Caesar's clemency; Brutus was one of Caesar's assassins; Dion of Syracuse, Plato's friend, tried to turn a tyranny into just rule. Each of the five paid for his stand, and Marcus, who himself now holds that power, records their names with gratitude.

Perhaps Severus only named aloud what Marcus already knew through Rusticus. Why an emperor needs such names we saw in [[01-11|1.11]]: knowing what makes power tyrannical is an inoculation against becoming a tyrant oneself.

Here too is one of Marcus's few statements of a political ideal: from Severus he received "the idea of a polity in which there is the same law for all, a polity administered with regard to equal rights and equal freedom of speech, and the idea of a kingly government which respects most of all the freedom of the governed." These words come from the vocabulary of Greek democracy: ἰσονομία is equality before the law, and ἰσηγορία, literally "equality of speech," the right of every citizen to speak in the assembly. Yet the sentence ends not with a republic but with a kingship: βασιλεία, which respects the freedom of the governed. Marcus does not dream of returning Rome to Cato's republic; another question occupies him: what the power already in his hands ought to be.

The second part of the passage is about Severus's character: steadiness in his regard for philosophy, generosity, hope, trust in the love of his friends. Severus said openly whom he condemned, and "his friends had no need to conjecture what he wished or did not wish, but it was quite plain." This is candor: with him no one had to guess.

**Commentary.**

The passage on his adoptive father, the emperor Antoninus Pius, is several times longer than any other. All the qualities Marcus gathered one by one from his grandfather, his mother and his teachers here converge in one man, who ruled Rome for twenty-three years and whom Marcus saw every day.

**How he decided.** Marcus begins with what distinguished Antoninus: "mildness of temper, and unchangeable resolution in the things which he had determined after due deliberation." Antoninus had a "habit of careful inquiry in all matters of deliberation," and "he never stopped his investigation through being satisfied with appearances which first present themselves"; he listened to anyone "who had anything to propose for the common weal."

**How he bore himself with people.** "He released his friends from all obligation to sup with him or to attend him of necessity when he went abroad," and those who had been away "always found him the same." He did not change friends on a whim, but was not "extravagant in his affection" either. He checked "popular applause and all flattery," and "he was most ready to give way without envy to those who possessed any particular faculty," and even "gave them his help, that each might enjoy reputation according to his deserts." For a man whose word carried so much weight, this is a rare trait: not to press, not to demand attendance, not to compete with those who know more.

**How he treated possessions and his own body.** "He did not take the bath at unseasonable hours; he was not fond of building houses, nor curious about what he ate, nor about the texture and colour of his clothes, nor about the beauty of his slaves." Then comes a sentence about where his clothes came from, Lorium and Lanuvium. The text is corrupt here, but the sense seems to be that the emperor wore plain local cloth. The goods "of which fortune gives an abundant supply" he used "without arrogance and without excusing himself; so that when he had them, he enjoyed them without affectation, and when he had them not, he did not want them." Marcus applies to him what Xenophon records of Socrates: "that he was able both to abstain from, and to enjoy, those things which many are too weak to abstain from, and cannot enjoy without excess."

**The illness of Maximus.** The passage breaks off on an episode we do not know: such a soul "he showed in the illness of Maximus." This is Claudius Maximus, the Stoic of the previous passage ([[01-15|1.15]]). What happened Marcus does not tell: the name is enough for him to recall the whole scene. The event is named but not described, and this is perhaps the clearest evidence that the notes are not addressed to a reader.

Of twenty-three years of rule Marcus records no campaign, law or victory; there remains only how he decided, how he bore himself, how he ate and dressed. For Marcus greatness is measured not by what a man accomplished but by what he remained while holding power.

In Book VI ([[06-30|6.30]]), in the same passage that says "Take care that thou art not made into a Caesar," Marcus writes: "Do everything as a disciple of Antoninus," and lists his traits once more. Antoninus remained his model.

## 1.17

![[01-17#Английский]]

**Commentary.**

The last passage is addressed to the gods. Marcus thanks them "for having good grandfathers, good parents, a good sister, good teachers"; for his mother: "though it was my mother's fate to die young, she spent the last years of her life with me"; for having known "Apollonius, Rusticus, Maximus"; for not falling, when he was drawn to philosophy, "into the hands of any sophist"; for his body, which "has held out so long in such a kind of life."

Some of the thanks are unexpected. One is for this: "that I did not make more proficiency in rhetoric, poetry, and the other studies," that is, for a failure. Another is for his adoptive brother Lucius Verus, a dissolute man, "who was able by his moral character to rouse me to vigilance over myself": a bad example can also be a lesson. A third is "that I have such a wife, so obedient, and so affectionate, and so simple." Ancient tradition made Faustina a profligate; modern historians regard much of this as slander. Marcus himself, as we see, thought otherwise.

The last words of the passage: "for all these things require the help of the gods and fortune" (θεῶν βοηθῶν καὶ τύχης δεῖται). Beneath them in the manuscript stands a note: "Among the Quadi at the Granua." The Quadi were a Germanic tribe beyond the Danube, where the war was being fought; the Granua was a river in their land. If the note belongs to Book I, then Marcus wrote his thanks for Antoninus's lesson, that a man can "bring himself very near to the fashion of a private person" even in a palace, in a military camp.

## The Book in Brief

**Key thought.** The first book draws a portrait of Marcus out of qualities received from others: from his family, his teachers, his friends, his adoptive father and, finally, from the gods. One thread runs through it: philosophy as the treatment of character ([[01-07|1.7]]), the ability to recognize tyranny ([[01-11|1.11]]), the ideal of a rule that respects the freedom of the governed ([[01-14|1.14]]), and the man who embodied that ideal ([[01-16|1.16]]). There are no doctrines here: the material of the exercise is people.

**Practice: notes to oneself (ὑπομνήματα).** The formula of the book is set by its first line: "From my grandfather Verus I learned good morals and the government of my temper" ([[01-01|1.1]]); in the Greek there is no verb, only "from whom" and "what." The exercise is to make such an inventory for oneself: a person and one quality or habit received from that person, or one thing that person helped one to give up, briefly, without a story and without thought of a reader. One line per person is enough; the list can be returned to and extended without fear of repetition. The last line, as in Marcus, speaks of what did not depend on people: "for all these things require the help of the gods and fortune" ([[01-17|1.17]]).
